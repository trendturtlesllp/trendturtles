Buyers paid ₹396.30 for one unit of a Nasdaq ETF with a real value of ₹118.14 on 18 September 2026, 3.35 times its value.

The fund is the Motilal Oswal Nasdaq Q50 ETF, traded as MONQ50. In two weeks its price rose 179% and its value did not, and trading rose with the price. Before you buy any ETF, check its value, not only its price.

By Mayank Gola, TrendTurtles  ·  9 min read  ·  Published: October 2026

Two weeks earlier, on 4 September, the same ETF cost ₹141.93 and held about the same value of shares, yet people kept buying as the price climbed. ₹1 lakh put in at the 18 September close was worth about ₹54,950 on 30 October, a fall of 45%. A buyer on 30 October was still paying 85% above the value. This gap, ETF price vs NAV, can be checked before you buy.

ETF Price vs NAV: An ETF Has Two Prices

An ETF (exchange traded fund) is a fund you buy and sell on the stock exchange, like a share. It holds many shares, or gold or silver. Each piece of the fund is called a unit. MONQ50 follows the Nasdaq Q-50 index, a list of US-listed shares, so it has to buy foreign shares.

The first price is the NAV (net asset value): the value of what the fund holds, for one unit. It is announced at the end of the day. The iNAV (indicative NAV) is an estimate of the same value during trading hours.

The second price is the market price, the one on your app. If it is above the NAV, the gap is called a premium. If it is below, the gap is called a discount.

Firms called market makers usually keep the two close. A market maker gets new units from the fund house (the company that runs the fund) and sells them on the exchange, which brings a high price back down. If new units cannot be made, a gap can open between price and value.

Why New Units Are Hard to Make

The reason is a legal limit on buying foreign shares. Mutual funds in India have an overseas investing limit of US$7 billion across the industry. The head of research at Bajaj Capital told Mint what this means: "additional units of an ETF cannot be created even if there is demand."

Motilal Oswal warned about this in December 2024, for MONQ50 and its Nasdaq 100 ETF. The fund house asked investors to check iNAV and use limit orders, according to INDmoney. A limit order sets the highest price you will pay.

Why the Gap Grew After 7 September 2026

Until September 2026, an ETF's daily price limit let it move only 20% either way from its NAV of two days earlier. By early September, MONQ50's price sat at this limit. On 4 September that was ₹141.93, which is 20% above its 2 September NAV of ₹118.28.

SEBI, the stock market regulator, then changed where this limit is counted from. It is now counted from the ETF's own closing price of the day before, not from its NAV. NSE applied this from 7 September 2026.

In simple words: each day's price limit now starts from yesterday's price, even if that price already holds a premium. So the price of a share ETF can rise by up to 20% every day, even if the NAV does not move. Gold and silver ETFs start with a 6% limit, which can be widened in steps during the day with no final cap. Mirae Asset told investors that a premium "can also go beyond the earlier 20% level", according to Cafemutual.

SEBI wants the daily price limit counted from the NAV again from 1 April 2027, once exchanges and fund houses solve the practical problems.

On 4 September the price could not move past its limit, and there were only 95 trades, all at ₹141.93. On 18 September there were 24,329 trades. The same units can be bought and sold many times in a day, so the better measure is units delivered to buyers' accounts: 17,832 on 4 September and 6,30,997 on 18 September, about 35 times as many. The NAV did not rise.

On 21 September the price touched ₹471.99 and fell to ₹317.04, and the most units of the period traded on 24 September, after the fall.

We cannot know why each person bought. Every trade also has a seller, and no source says who these buyers were. When a price keeps rising fast, look at the value.

The premium fell from 235.5% to 68.3% in three trading days. It then closed at 56.4% on 29 September and 111.8% on 14 October. A director at Anand Rathi Wealth told Mint the September fall was "driven by the unwinding of this premium, rather than a collapse in the underlying US stocks". In simple words: the price fell because the extra paid above value shrank, not because the shares fell.

Silver ETFs, January 2026: the price stayed lower after the value recovered

Silver ETFs also traded above their value, and that gap closed quickly. After strong returns over one year, IIFL reported, small and medium investors rushed into silver ETFs. On 21 January 2026, the seven we checked, which include the four largest, closed 3% to 7% above their NAV. Not every silver ETF moved this much. The NAV and the closing price are not set at the same moment, so part of this gap may be timing. On 22 January they closed 10% to 16% lower, while their NAVs fell 6% to 7%. All seven ended that day below NAV, and they were still below it on 23 January. These numbers come from NSE's price files for 21 January and 22 January.

Nippon India's silver ETF, SILVERBEES, shows what this meant. By 23 January its NAV was back above the 21 January level, at ₹299.75 against ₹298.67. Its price was still 5.8% lower, at ₹292.73 against ₹310.68. Sources: NSE's price file and AMFI's NAV history.

Mirae Asset NYSE FANG+ ETF, 2024: a 26.3% premium, then a small loss

This ETF holds big US technology companies. In April 2024 it traded at a 26.3% premium. ₹1 lakh invested then was worth about ₹97,200 by the end of June, 5paisa reported, citing Value Research, because price and NAV had nearly met. The loss was small only because the fund's value rose about 21% in those months, by our arithmetic from its NAV of ₹76.91 on 19 April and ₹93.43 on 28 June, from AMFI data.

What a Premium Costs You

The premium is money you pay above the value of the shares. At ₹217.76 for ₹117.63 of shares, ₹100.13 of the price is premium. That is 46% of what you pay. If the premium goes to zero and the shares do not move, you lose that 46%.

Many people have paid some premium. BusinessLine estimates that ₹3,094 crore of the ₹10,440 crore traded so far in 2026 in six international ETFs was above NAV. We found no reported case of a named person who lost money this way.

What we did find, on Zerodha's public forum, is people who could not understand the gap. In May 2025, one user saw the Nasdaq 100 index down 4.4% from its high and an ETF on that index down 20%, and asked why. Another could not find the iNAV on the fund house's website.

You can also pay a premium without buying the ETF yourself. A fund of funds is a fund that invests in other funds. Some hold these ETFs and value them at the exchange price, 5paisa reports, so their NAV includes the premium.

How to Check an ETF's Premium

These six steps work for any ETF, and the first three matter most.

  1. Compare the price rise with the NAV rise. If the price has risen fast, ask if the NAV rose with it. Between 4 and 18 September, MONQ50's price rose 179% and its NAV fell 0.5%.
  2. Find the iNAV before you order. Look on the fund house's ETF page or your broker's app. For MONQ50 and MON100, Motilal Oswal says the iNAV is calculated "considering only currency movement from the previous day's closing NAV". So for these two it follows the dollar, not the day's share prices. Treat it as a rough guide.
  3. Work out two numbers. The premium is price minus iNAV, divided by iNAV. The share of the price that is premium is price minus iNAV, divided by price. At a price of ₹120 and an iNAV of ₹100, the premium is 20% and 16.7% of the price is premium.
  4. Decide if you accept losing that share. Remember the shares can fall as well. Mirae Asset tells buyers of its overseas ETFs to compare price and iNAV and avoid large premiums. No rule says what "large" means, so the decision is yours.
  5. Use a limit order. A market order takes the best price available at that moment. Zerodha's Varsity says to always use a limit order after checking the iNAV.
  6. Check what your fund of funds holds. Its monthly factsheet lists its holdings. If it holds an ETF priced above its NAV, part of your NAV may be that premium.

If you already hold one: use step 3 to see how much of today's price is premium. We do not tell anyone to buy, hold or sell. A SEBI-registered adviser can look at your own position.

If you are buying your first ETF on an app: the price on the screen is not the value of the fund, so do steps 1 to 3 first.

If you invest a fixed amount every month: check the premium before you set up the order, and again from time to time.

If you saw a big rise in an ETF and want to buy: start with step 1, before you look at anything else.

Key Takeaways

  • When an ETF's price rises fast, check if its NAV rose too.
  • A premium is money above the value of the shares. If it shrinks, you lose money even when the shares do not fall.
  • When new units are hard to make, a premium can stay wide or fall suddenly.
  • Since 7 September 2026, an ETF's daily price limit is counted from its previous closing price, so the premium can keep growing day after day.
  • For any ETF, check the iNAV before you order and work out what share of the price is premium.
