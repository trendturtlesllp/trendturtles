Home › Blog › ETF or Index Fund

Index funds charge higher fees than ETFs. Tata's Nifty 50 index fund charges 0.26% a year and its Nifty 50 ETF, 0.09%.

In 19 of 20 fund companies that run both an index fund and an ETF, the index fund charges more, but the ETF adds a broker's charges, and its price is dependent on market fluctuations.

Source: the yearly fee list of AMFI, the group of fund companies, November 2026.

By Mayank Gola, TrendTurtles  ·  10 min read  ·  Published: November 2026

ETF or index fund, which is better? Tata Mutual Fund runs both on the Nifty 50, the list of India's 50 biggest companies on NSE, the National Stock Exchange. Its ETF charges the lower fee, but is it cheaper once the broker and the price are added?

The costs start with how you buy each one.

1. Same Shares, Two Ways to Buy

An index fund is a mutual fund that buys only the shares of an index, such as the Nifty 50. You buy its units, the small equal pieces a fund is divided into, from the fund company, on its website or a fund app. You pay the NAV, the real value of one unit, worked out once a day after the market closes. If your money reaches the fund by 3 pm, you get that day's NAV. When you sell, the fund company pays you the NAV. You do not need a demat account.

An ETF, or exchange traded fund, is also a mutual fund that buys the same index shares. But you buy and sell it on your stock app, like a share. For this you need a broker, the firm that runs the app, and a demat account, where the broker keeps your units. You pay the price at which someone is ready to sell, so it can be a little above or below the NAV.

To help you compare, the exchange shows the iNAV, the live value of one unit, at most 15 seconds behind the market. What does each fund cost you every year?

2. The Yearly Fee: Index Funds Charge More

Every fund takes a yearly fee from your money, called the expense ratio. It is cut from the fund's value every day, so you never see a bill. An index fund has two plans. The direct plan is bought straight from the fund company, and the word "Direct" is in its name. The regular plan is bought through an agent, who is paid from the fee, so it costs more: Tata's regular plan charges twice its direct plan.

For every ₹100 you hold, Tata's direct plan takes 26 paise a year and its ETF takes 9 paise. Here is the fee for the Nifty 50 funds of eight fund companies:

JioBlackRock is the only one where the ETF costs more than the index fund. In Axis and Motilal Oswal, the direct plan and the ETF charge almost the same. So check the fee of the exact fund, not the type. What else do you pay to buy, sell and hold an ETF?

3. The Other Costs: Buying, Selling and Holding

An ETF is bought through a broker, so it has a broker's charges. Each broker sets its own. The ETF column below uses the published charges of Zerodha, a broker, as one example. An index fund's direct plan has no broker, so it has no broker's charges.

When you sell, both also pay a very small tax called STT, ₹1 on every ₹1 lakh (ETF, index fund). On an ETF, GST of ₹18 on every ₹100 of fees is added to the broker's, exchange's and SEBI's fees. The ₹15.34 already includes it. Tata's ETF has no exit fee.

The ₹15.34 is the same whether you sell a small amount or a large one. If you sell just 1 unit of Tata's ETF, worth about ₹273, the fee takes about ₹5.60 of every ₹100 you get. On a sale of many units, it is a tiny part. These costs are all written in a list. One cost is not: the price.

4. The Price You Get, and Whether You Can Sell

When you sell an ETF, you get what a buyer pays. On 16 November 2026, Tata's ETF ended the day at ₹273.38, while one unit was worth ₹275.90. A seller at that price got ₹2.52 less per unit than its value. On 13 and 17 November, the gap was about half of that, and on 18 November only 16 paise.

Put that next to the fee. Tata's ETF saves you 17 paise a year on every ₹100, compared with its index fund's direct plan. On 16 November, a seller lost about 91 paise on every ₹100. That one sale cost more than 5 years of the fee saving.

The gap is usually smaller in an ETF that many people trade. 22 ETFs follow the same Nifty 50. On 17 November, Nippon India's Nifty 50 ETF was traded for ₹279 crore, and Edelweiss's for only about ₹80,000. Over those four days, Nippon's ETF, with over 43,000 trades a day, stayed closer to its value than Tata's, which had fewer than 200. A 2025 Value Research study found that of 62 ETFs of big Indian companies, just 3 got about 70% of all trading. Our gold ETF article shows why the number of trades matters.

Sometimes you cannot sell at all. In 2024, an investor wrote on a Zerodha forum that an order in SBI's Nifty Next 50 ETF did not go through, because too few people were trading it. On 1 February 2026, the Budget day, silver ETFs hit the lowest price the exchange allowed that day, and investors were "unable to sell", Value Research reported.

For such times, ETFs have one safety rule. If, for 7 market days in a row, an ETF ends the day more than ₹1 below its value on every ₹100, or for 3 market days in a row nobody offers to buy or sell it, you can sell it straight back to the fund company at the NAV, with no exit fee.

The price can also be above the value, and then the buyer pays extra. Value Research found that ETFs of foreign shares were more than ₹5 away from their value on every ₹100, on about 45 of every 100 days the market was open, while ETFs of India's biggest companies rarely were. Among funds that follow the Nasdaq 100, a list of US companies, ₹100 put for one year in a fund that buys an Indian Nasdaq 100 ETF grew to about ₹153, while in four others it grew to about ₹130. The extra came only from that ETF's price rising above its value, which its buyers paid for, Value Research reported in September 2026. Our ETF price article shows how buyers lose when that gap closes. So how do the two compare, side by side?

Both are taxed alike. For funds that hold Indian shares, if you sell within 12 months, the tax is ₹20 on every ₹100 of gain. After 12 months, the first ₹1.25 lakh of gain in a year is tax-free, and after that the tax is ₹12.50 on every ₹100 of gain.

So the ETF's lower yearly fee can be lost to its other costs and its price, most of all on small amounts and in ETFs that few people trade. How do you get in and out of each at the lowest cost?

6. The Cheaper Way In and Out

  1. Compare the yearly fee. Open AMFI's fee page, pick the fund company and the fund, and read the fee for the direct plan, the regular plan and the ETF.
  2. For an index fund, buy the direct plan and wait out the exit fee. Check that "Direct" is in the fund's name before you pay. On the fund's page, read how many days the exit fee applies, and do not sell before then.
  3. For an ETF, pick one that many people trade, and check its price against its iNAV. Open NSE's ETF page, find the ETF, and compare its price with its iNAV. The page also shows how many units were traded that day.
  4. Set your own price for an ETF. On your stock app, choose a limit order: you type the price, and the trade happens only at that price or a better one (lower when you buy, higher when you sell). Type a price close to the iNAV.
  5. Check your broker's charges. On your broker's charges page, find the fee to buy, the fee for each sale and the yearly demat fee. Sell in one go rather than in small parts, as each sale pays the fixed fee again.
  6. If your ETF stays more than ₹1 below its value on every ₹100 for 7 market days, ask the fund company to buy your units back at the NAV, under the rule in section 4.

If you invest a small amount every month: do steps 1, 2 and 5, because a fixed fee is a bigger part of a small amount.

If you invest a large amount and hold it for years: do steps 1, 3 and 4, because the yearly fee adds up over the years.

If you have no demat account: an index fund needs none; an ETF adds step 5's demat fee.

Key Takeaways

  • One sale below the value can wipe out years of an ETF's fee saving.
  • Not every ETF is cheaper, and not every ETF is easy to sell.
  • Check the fee, the iNAV and your broker's charges before you choose.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is an ETF a mutual fund?

Yes. An ETF is a mutual fund whose units are bought and sold on the stock exchange (see section 1).

Are an ETF and an index fund on the same index the same?

They hold the same shares, but you buy them differently and pay different costs (see section 5).

Does an ETF or an index fund follow the index more closely?

Both must stay close to it. Tracking error is how far a fund's daily return moves away from the index over a year, and SEBI caps it at 2% for both. AMFI shows each fund's figure on its tracking error page.

Do I need a demat account for an index fund?

No. You can buy an index fund straight from the fund company; only an ETF needs a demat account (see section 1).
