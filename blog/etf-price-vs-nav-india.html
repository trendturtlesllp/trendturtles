By Mayank Gola, TrendTurtles  ·  17 min read  ·  Published: October 2026  ·  Updated: 2 November 2026

On 18 September 2026, one unit of the Motilal Oswal Nasdaq Q50 ETF ended the day at ₹396.30 on the stock market. The real value of that unit was only ₹118.14. Two weeks earlier, the same unit cost ₹141.93, and its real value was ₹118.74. NAV means the real value of 1 unit. This article explains why ETF prices are different from NAV.

If you put ₹1 lakh into this ETF on 18 September, your money was worth about ₹54,950 on 30 October. You lost 45%, even though the real value remained almost the same. A buyer on 30 October still paid 85% more than the real value.

How can a price go so far above the real value? To see this, you first need to know that an ETF has a price and a real value. They are two different numbers.

Why ETF Prices Are Different From NAV: A Price and a Real Value

ETF stands for exchange traded fund. A fund collects money from many people and buys things like shares, gold or silver. A share is a small part of a company. An ETF is sold in small pieces called units, and you buy these units on the stock market through an app.

Each unit has a real value. The fund adds up what it owns, takes away what it owes, and divides this by the number of units. Say a fund owns shares worth ₹1,000 and it has 10 units. The real value of 1 unit is ₹100. This real value is called the NAV (net asset value).

Each unit also has a price. This is the price you see on your app. Buyers and sellers decide this price. When many people want to buy, the price goes up.

So you can pay more than the real value. If you pay ₹200 for something worth ₹100, the extra ₹100 is called the premium. Here the premium is 100%, because you paid 100% more than the real value.

Most of the time, an ETF's premium stays small. The next section shows why.

Why the Price Usually Stays Close to the Real Value

When too many people want to buy an ETF, its price goes up. To bring the price down, more units must come up for sale. Big firms called market makers bring these extra units. Here is how they do it.

Say the real value is ₹100 and the price goes up to ₹120. A market maker gets new units from the fund house, the company that runs the fund, at about ₹100. You cannot do this on your app, because new units are made only in very large lots. For the Nasdaq ETF in this article, one lot is 6,00,000 units. The market maker sells these units on the stock market at the higher price and keeps the difference. Now more units are for sale, so buyers do not need to pay ₹120. The price comes down towards ₹100.

This works only if new units can be made.

Now come back to the Nasdaq ETF from the start of this article. It buys shares of US companies. To make new units, the fund must buy more of these shares. So it must send money to the US, and India limits this money. Value Research, a fund research firm, says no new units of ETFs like this one have been made since April 2024. So no new units came in to bring the price down. So the price depended only on how many people wanted to buy and sell. The stock market also has a limit on how far a price can move in one day. From April 2026, this ETF's price had reached this limit, so it could not go higher. We worked out the April date from NSE and AMFI data. Then, on 7 September 2026, this daily limit changed. The next section shows how the limit worked and what changed.

Why the Premium Grew After 7 September 2026

The stock market has a limit on how much an ETF's price can move in one day. This is called the daily price limit. On any day, the price cannot go above the top limit or below the bottom limit.

Until September 2026, this limit came from the NAV. Remember, the NAV is the real value of 1 unit. The price could move only 20% above or below the NAV of two days earlier. Say that NAV was ₹100. Then the top limit was ₹120. This stopped the premium from going far above 20%. On 4 September, this ETF's top limit was ₹141.93. We calculated this: it is 20% above the 2 September NAV of ₹118.28. The price reached this limit and could not go higher.

Then SEBI, the body that makes rules for the stock market, changed the rule. The new rule started on 7 September 2026. For ETFs that buy shares, like this one, the limit is still up to 20%. But it now comes from yesterday's closing price, not from the NAV. The closing price is the average price of the last 30 minutes of the day.

Here is what that means. Say the closing price is ₹200 and the real value is ₹120. The next day the price can go up to ₹240. If it closes at ₹240, the limit for the day after is ₹288. So the price can rise by up to 20% every day, even if the real value does not change.

This is what happened to this ETF. Its name on the stock market is MONQ50. In the table, the closing price and the units bought and sold are from NSE, the stock exchange. The NAV is from AMFI, the group of fund companies. The premium shows how much the price is above the NAV, in percent.

Did people really buy at these high prices? Yes. The table counts a unit again each time it is sold. So we also counted only the units buyers did not sell the same day. That number was 17,832 on 4 September and 6,30,997 on 18 September, about 35 times as many. Be careful with this 35 times. It looks big because the number on 4 September was small. That day the price was stuck at its top limit, and few units were bought. We also do not know why each person bought.

Then the price fell. By 23 September it was ₹202.92. Mint, a business newspaper, spoke to a director at Anand Rathi Wealth, a financial firm. The director said the fall was "driven by the unwinding of this premium, rather than a collapse in the underlying US stocks". In simple words: the value of the US shares inside the ETF remained almost the same. The price fell because buyers stopped paying so much extra. On 7 October the price was ₹258.57, and on 30 October it was ₹217.76. Both prices were still far above the NAV.

So a buyer can pay far more than the real value, and the price can fall even when the shares do not fall. What does this mean for your money?

What a Premium Means for Your Money

Remember the example. You pay ₹200 for something worth ₹100. The extra ₹100 gets you nothing more. You still own something worth ₹100.

To get your money out, you usually must sell to another buyer on the stock market. Until the real value reaches ₹200, you can make a profit only if this next buyer also pays extra. The premium can also become smaller while the real value rises. Say the real value rises from ₹100 to ₹110, but the price falls from ₹200 to ₹150. The real value went up, but if you sell at ₹150, you lose ₹50.

Now look at the Nasdaq ETF on 30 October. The price was ₹217.76 and the real value was ₹117.63. Say you bought on that day, at ₹217.76. The real value has to rise about 85% just to reach what you paid. If the premium goes away first and the real value does not change, the price falls from ₹217.76 to ₹117.63. That is ₹100.13 less, which is about 46% of what you paid.

This ETF is not the only one. Other ETFs have also had a premium that later became smaller. The reasons were not always the same.

Other ETFs Have Had a Premium Before

Why Silver ETF Prices Fell More Than Their NAV, January 2026

A silver ETF is a fund that buys silver. Silver ETFs had gone up a lot in one year. Many people with small and medium amounts of money then rushed to buy them, IIFL reported. IIFL is a company that buys and sells shares for people.

We checked seven silver ETFs. At the end of the day on 21 January 2026, their prices were 3% to 7% above their NAV. The next day, silver fell and their NAVs fell 6% to 7%. But their prices fell 10% to 16%. The prices of some other silver ETFs fell less. Now go back to the premium of 3% to 7% on 21 January. This number is not exact. The silver price changes during the day. The ETF's closing price and the NAV may use the silver price from two different times of the day. These numbers are from NSE (21 January, 22 January) and AMFI. Buyers lost money because silver fell, and they also lost the extra money they had paid. In the next case, buyers lost money even though the NAV rose.

Mirae Asset NYSE FANG+ ETF, 2024

This ETF buys foreign shares. In January 2024, Mirae Asset, the fund house that runs it, stopped taking money from large investors for new units. This was for its ETFs that buy foreign shares. Market makers could still get new units until March. Mint reported in April 2024 that market makers could no longer buy new units from the fund house. This kept the prices of these ETFs above their NAV. That month, this ETF's price was 26.3% above its NAV. If you put in ₹1 lakh then, your money was worth about ₹97,200 by the end of June. 5paisa, a company that buys and sells shares for people, reported this, using numbers from Value Research.

You lost money even though the NAV rose from ₹76.91 to ₹93.43. We calculated that this rise is about 21%. You had paid 26.3% extra, and almost all of it went away. We now have three cases, so let us put them side by side.

The reason can change, but the check is the same. Here is what to do before you buy any ETF.

How to Check an ETF Before You Buy

  1. See if the NAV rose with the price. If the price has gone up fast, check if the NAV also went up. The NAV is on the website of the fund house, the company that runs the fund. Between 4 and 18 September, the Nasdaq Q50 ETF's price rose 179%, and its NAV fell 0.5%. So all of this price rise was extra money.
  2. Find the iNAV. The fund announces the NAV only at the end of the day, but you buy during the day. So during the day you look at the iNAV, an estimate of the NAV. Look on the fund house's website, or on your app if it shows the iNAV. For some ETFs this number is very rough. The questions at the end of this article explain why.
  3. Calculate the extra money. Say the price is ₹120 and the iNAV is ₹100. The extra money is ₹20. Divide ₹20 by the iNAV and multiply by 100. You get a 20% premium. Now divide ₹20 by the price, ₹120, and multiply by 100. You get 16.7%. This second number is the part of your money you can lose if the premium goes away. For the Nasdaq ETF on 30 October, these two numbers were 85.1% and about 46%.
  4. Use a limit order. When you buy, the app asks how you want to buy. A market order buys at the price sellers ask now. A limit order lets you set the highest price you will pay. Say you set ₹105 and sellers ask ₹120. Your order will not buy at ₹120. It will buy only if the price comes down to ₹105 or less. So a limit order stops you from paying more than you decided. Varsity, the learning site of Zerodha, says to check the iNAV and always use a limit order. Zerodha is a company that buys and sells shares for people.
  5. Decide if you accept the risk. The shares in the fund can also fall, so you can lose more than the extra money you paid. No rule says how much premium is too much, so you must decide.

If you already own an ETF: use step 3 to see how much of today's price is extra money. We do not tell anyone to buy, keep or sell. An adviser registered with SEBI can look at what you own.

If you are buying your first ETF: the price on the screen is not the real value. Do steps 1 to 3 first.

If you put in money every month: check the premium before you start, and then check it every month, because it can change.

Key Takeaways

  • The premium is the extra money you pay above the real value of 1 unit, the NAV.
  • Until the real value reaches what you paid, you can make a profit only if the next buyer also pays extra. If you sell for less than you paid, you lose money, even when the real value rises.
  • For ETFs that buy shares, the daily price limit has come from yesterday's closing price since 7 September 2026. So the premium can grow every day.
  • Before you buy any ETF, check the iNAV and calculate how much extra you are paying.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why are ETF prices different from the NAV?

ETF prices are different from the NAV because buyers and sellers decide the price on the stock market, but the NAV comes from the value of what the fund owns. The NAV is the real value of 1 unit. When many people want to buy, the price can go above the NAV.

What is NAV in an ETF?

NAV stands for net asset value. It is the real value of 1 unit of the fund. The fund adds up what it owns, takes away what it owes, and divides this by the number of units. It announces the NAV at the end of the day.

What is iNAV?

iNAV stands for indicative net asset value. It is an estimate of the NAV during the day, while the stock market is open. The NAV is the real value of 1 unit of an ETF, and the fund announces it at the end of the day. Motilal Oswal, a company that runs funds, has ETFs called MONQ50 and MON100. These two ETFs buy US shares. For these two, it says the iNAV is calculated "considering only currency movement from the previous day's closing NAV". In simple words: their iNAV changes only when the price of 1 US dollar in rupees changes. It does not follow the US shares inside the fund during the day. So it is only a rough number.

Why are gold ETF prices different from the NAV?

Gold ETF prices are different from the NAV because buyers and sellers decide the price, but the NAV comes from the gold the fund owns. A gold ETF is a fund that buys gold. The NAV is the real value of 1 unit. On 21 January 2026, Nippon India's gold ETF, GOLDBEES, ended the day at ₹134.97, with an NAV of ₹127.02. That is about 6% above its NAV. The next day it ended at ₹124.34, with an NAV of ₹124.57. So the price had come down to just below the NAV.

What is MONQ50?

MONQ50 is the name of the Motilal Oswal Nasdaq Q50 ETF on the stock market. It buys shares of US companies on a list called the Nasdaq Q-50 index.

What is a market maker?

A market maker is a big firm that buys and sells ETF units on the stock market. An ETF is sold in small pieces called units, and the real value of 1 unit is called the NAV. When the price goes above the NAV, the market maker gets new units from the fund house, the company that runs the fund. It pays about the NAV and sells the units at the higher price. More units for sale bring the price down.

Why can't new units of MONQ50 be made?

MONQ50 is the Motilal Oswal Nasdaq Q50 ETF, and it buys shares of US companies. To make new units, the fund must send money to the US to buy more shares. India limits this money. A mutual fund collects money from many people, and an ETF is one kind of mutual fund. All mutual funds in India together can send only 7 billion US dollars. That is 700 crore US dollars. India has more than one limit like this, and reports differ on exactly which one stopped MONQ50.

Is the daily price limit the same for every ETF?

No. The daily price limit is how far an ETF's price can move in one day. For ETFs that buy shares, the limit is up to 20%. Gold and silver ETFs start with a 6% limit, which can be increased in steps during the day. Since 7 September 2026, the limit for ETFs that buy shares has come from yesterday's closing price. The closing price is the average price of the last 30 minutes of the day. Say the closing price is ₹200. The next day the price can go up to ₹240.

The old limit came from the NAV of two days earlier. SEBI, the body that makes rules for the stock market, wants the limit to come from the NAV again. But it wants yesterday's NAV, not the NAV of two days earlier. SEBI wants this to start on 1 April 2027. First, stock exchanges and fund companies must solve some practical problems.

Can a fund of funds also have a premium?

Yes. A fund of funds is a fund that buys other funds, such as ETFs. 5paisa, a company that buys and sells shares for people, reports that some of these funds use an ETF's price, not its NAV, when they add up what they own. The NAV is the real value of 1 unit. So part of your fund's value may be premium, the extra money paid above the real value. Read the fund's monthly report to see what it owns.
