Home › Blog › Silver ETF or Physical Silver

Physical silver is mostly 6% to 36% more expensive than its real value. Silver ETF is found to be the cheapest, at a yearly cost of 0.35% to 0.60%.

It is expensive because of 3% GST, making and shipping charges, the seller's profit, and the gap between buying and selling price. Silver ETF has real silver, managed by a fund house and regulated by SEBI.

Source: prices of Augmont, SafeGold and Bangalore Refinery on 23 and 24 November 2026, the silver rate of IBJA, the bullion dealers' association, and NSE prices on 25 November 2026.

By Mayank Gola, TrendTurtles  ·  18 min read  ·  Published: November 2026

Let's take the prices of 24 November 2026 as an example. A buyer who bought a 10 g silver coin from Augmont's online shop that day paid Rs. 1,949. The real value of its silver was about Rs. 1,520, at that morning's wholesale rate, the price at which dealers trade large amounts. So the buyer paid about Rs. 429 more than the real value, about Rs. 28 on every Rs. 100.

A silver ETF, or exchange traded fund, holds the same kind of silver, but it is bought on a share-buying app. This is one part of our series on what a buyer really gets with an ETF; the main article is ETF or index fund. First, where does the extra money on a coin go?

1. Silver ETF vs Physical Silver: What Each Costs

There are four ways to buy silver: coins and bars, a silver app (also called digital silver, where the app says it keeps the silver for the buyer), a silver ETF, or a silver fund that buys the ETF for the buyer.

Coins and bars

Augmont, a company that sells silver coins and bars, lists each part of its coin price in its online shop's price list. For the 10 g coin, it adds Rs. 3 a gram for making, Rs. 180 for shipping and Rs. 70 for the hallmark, a purity stamp, and packing. Then it adds a 4% margin, its own profit, and then 3% GST. Shipping and packing are a fixed amount, not charged per gram. So a small coin carries much more extra cost per gram.

Augmont's prices include GST. The refinery's list says its prices include tax, but one note on the page says GST is extra; if it is extra, its coins cost more than shown.

Online prices are higher because they add shipping and packing. Jewellers' gift coins cost even more: a 20 g CaratLane coin cost about Rs. 56 more on every Rs. 100 of silver.

One shop's terms say it plainly: "GST will not be refunded", and making charges are not paid back either. So the extra price is the first loss. Selling brings the second.

Selling it back

Silver apps show two prices: the price at which they sell, and a lower price at which they buy back. Take 24 November 2026 again. Someone who sold silver back on Augmont's app got about Rs. 94 for silver it sold at Rs. 100, before GST. On SafeGold, the same sale got about Rs. 96 (buy and sell prices). With the 3% GST paid at purchase, app silver sold back the same day returned about Rs. 9 less on every Rs. 100 at Augmont and Rs. 7 less at SafeGold. The gap stayed the same all day, even when the price fell.

Bangalore Refinery, a company that makes silver bars, shows both prices on its price list too. For example, a buyer of a 1 kg bar there on 24 November paid Rs. 1,65,520, and its buyback rate was Rs. 1,52,500 a kg, about Rs. 8 less on every Rs. 100. A buyer of its 10 g coin at the counter that day paid Rs. 1,727, but 10 g bought back at that rate is Rs. 1,525, about Rs. 12 less on every Rs. 100. Its page says the real buyback is that rate "minus certain percentage". So the real loss is bigger.

So on a small coin, a buyer can lose twice: the extra price when buying, and the lower price when selling. What does a silver ETF cost instead?

Silver ETF

A silver ETF is a fund that buys silver bars and splits them into units. One unit of Nippon India's silver ETF is about 1 gram of silver. Units are bought and sold on NSE, the National Stock Exchange, through a share-buying app. For this a buyer needs a broker, the company that runs the app, and a demat account, where the units are kept.

The fund takes a yearly fee from the silver's value. Across silver ETFs, it is 0.35% to 0.60% a year, or Rs. 35 to Rs. 60 a year on Rs. 10,000 (AMFI fee list, November 2026). No bill is shown; it is cut from the fund every day.

The broker also charges for each buy and sell. To buy Rs. 10,000 of a silver ETF and later sell it, the charges came to about Rs. 18 on Zerodha and about Rs. 49 on Groww, from their published charges. The biggest part is a fixed fee on each sale, Rs. 15 to Rs. 20.

On NSE, there is always a best price someone will pay and a best price someone will sell at. Someone who buys and sells at once loses that gap. For example, we read both prices for 14 silver ETFs on the morning of 25 November 2026. The gap was under Rs. 5 on Rs. 10,000 in Nippon India, ICICI, HDFC, SBI and Aditya Birla, from NSE prices shown on Groww at 10:35 am. Small ETFs had wider gaps, and very few units on offer: Bandhan's gap was about Rs. 30 on Rs. 10,000.

Silver ETF vs Silver Fund of Funds (FoF)

A silver fund of funds (FoF) is a mutual fund, bought from the fund company, that buys a silver ETF for the buyer. No demat account is needed, and a buyer can start a SIP, a fixed monthly investment, from Rs. 100 a month in Nippon India's. But it charges its own fee on top of the ETF's fee. Together it is about Rs. 56 to Rs. 80 a year on Rs. 10,000 in the direct plan, bought straight from the fund company, and about Rs. 100 to Rs. 112 in the regular plan, which is sold through an agent (AMFI fee list).

In the three years to 19 November 2026, Nippon India's silver FoF earned about 50 paise to 90 paise less a year on every Rs. 100 than its ETF, in the direct and regular plans (AMFI, 19 November 2026). The FoF earned less, and it charges a higher fee.

On Rs. 10,000 worth of silver bought as 10 g coins at the refinery counter, the extra is about Rs. 1,030, paid once. An ETF fee of Rs. 60 a year takes about 17 years to add up to that. For Augmont's online coins, it takes about 50 years. But an ETF has one more cost: the gap between its price and its silver's value.

2. Silver ETF Price vs NAV: Why the Price Can Be More Than the Silver's Value

A coin's silver is in the buyer's hand. An ETF's silver is in a vault, so the fund works out its value. That value is called the NAV, the value of one unit, set once a day from India's silver price. During the day, the exchange also shows the iNAV, the live value of one unit.

The price paid on the app is different. It is set by people buying and selling on NSE. Firms called market makers keep it close to the NAV, because they earn from the gap: when the price rises above it, they make new units by giving the fund real silver, and sell those units. When India runs short of silver, they cannot make units fast enough, and the price goes above the silver's value.

Take 9 October 2025 as an example. Before April 2026, funds worked out the NAV from the world silver price. India was short of silver, and Kotak Mutual Fund said silver in India cost 10% to 12% more than abroad. That day, HDFC Silver ETF's day-end price was about Rs. 107 for Rs. 100 of silver. So a buyer who paid Rs. 10,000 at that price paid about Rs. 645 above the real value. Ten market days later, that buyer had lost 14.4%, while the silver inside fell 7.5% (NSE prices, AMFI NAV history). In the same week, several fund companies stopped taking new one-time investments into their silver FoFs for about two weeks (NSE notice when SBI's FoF reopened), to keep buyers from paying the extra. A FoF's value comes from the ETF's day-end price. So when the ETF's price runs above its silver, the FoF's does too (SEBI rules).

Since April 2026, the gap has been small. India's silver price now sets every fund's NAV, under a rule of SEBI, the share market regulator, from 1 April 2026 (SEBI circular). Nippon India's silver ETF never closed more than about Rs. 113 above its NAV on Rs. 10,000, and on some days it closed up to about Rs. 170 below (NSE prices and AMFI NAV history for Nippon India Mutual Fund, our count). A buyer on a low day got more silver; a seller got less.

There is also a safety rule for when the price stays too low. If an ETF's day-end price stays more than Rs. 1 below its NAV on every Rs. 100 for 7 market days in a row, or NSE shows no price for it for 3 market days in a row, investors can sell their units back to the fund company at the NAV. There is no exit fee, a charge for selling early (SEBI rules, 20 March 2026). Even at the right price, the silver itself moves a lot.

3. Why Silver ETF Falls

Since February 2022, Nippon India's silver ETF fell on almost half of all market days, and fell more than 2% on about 1 day in 10 (AMFI NAV history for Nippon India Mutual Fund, our count). On a normal day it moved about twice as much as Nippon India's gold ETF.

Silver can fall for reasons far from India. Here are three examples, from news reports:

  • 30 January 2026: silver prices around the world fell sharply after a new head was named for the US central bank and the US dollar rose. Nippon India's silver ETF closed 18.6% lower that day (Business Today, NSE prices).
  • 23 October 2026: India allowed 6,500 tonnes of silver imports. More silver coming in meant a lower price, and silver ETFs fell about 4%.
  • 16 November 2026: reports blamed a stronger dollar, high US interest rates and weak demand from industry, which uses silver in solar panels and electronics (Business Today).

People who bought in a rush lost the most. DSP Mutual Fund found that silver rose 98% in the year to 31 July 2026, but the average silver ETF investor earned about 18%, because much of the money came in late. The biggest month for new money was January 2026, near the top (Business Today, 5 September 2026). Rs. 10,000 put into Nippon India's silver ETF at its top on 29 January 2026 was worth Rs. 4,053 on 20 November 2026 (AMFI NAV history for Nippon India Mutual Fund, our calculation).

Over longer periods, the result depends on the start date. Rs. 10,000 put in on 20 November 2025 was Rs. 9,823 on 20 November 2026, a day the silver price fell about 5%. Rs. 10,000 put in on 20 November 2023 was Rs. 20,323 (same source). No silver ETF in India is five years old yet. Through all these ups and downs, what does an investor own?

4. Is Silver ETF Safe? What Investors Really Own

Real silver. SEBI's rules say a silver ETF must keep at least 95% of its money in silver and silver futures, contracts to buy silver later. The bars must be 99.9% pure, 30 kg each, and the fund's auditors, outside checkers of its accounts, must check them every six months (SEBI rules, 20 March 2026). Nippon India's silver ETF held 98.44% in silver bars on 30 September 2026.

The silver does not belong to the fund company. A trustee company holds it for the people who own units, and a custodian, a firm paid to guard it, keeps it in a vault. If a fund is closed, its silver is sold, its bills are paid, and the rest goes to the investors (SEBI rules for mutual funds). An ETF becomes worth nothing only if silver itself does.

But the silver cannot be taken home. Only very large trades, above Rs. 25 crore, can be swapped for silver with the fund company. Banks also do not lend on silver ETF units. RBI's rules, from the Reserve Bank of India, allow loans on silver jewellery and coins, but not on things whose value comes from silver bars, like ETF units. Some broker apps accept units as a deposit for trading, but that is not a loan.

Coins are in the buyer's hand, but nobody checks them. Hallmarking of silver is still optional in India. If a piece is hallmarked, it must carry a 6-digit HUID code, a number for that one piece, which can be checked in the BIS CARE app of the Bureau of Indian Standards. Silver apps have no regulator either, a government body that watches them. SEBI warned in November 2025 that digital gold is outside its watch. Its warning names gold, but digital silver is sold the same way. And a sale is taxed.

5. Silver ETF Tax: Lower Rate After 12 Months for an ETF, 24 for Coins

Profit on a silver ETF sold after 12 months is taxed at 12.5%. On a silver FoF, coins, bars and app silver, the wait is 24 months for the same 12.5% rate. Sold earlier, the profit is added to the seller's income and taxed at that income-tax rate (Income-tax Act 2025, sections 2(101) and 197).

The first Rs. 1.25 lakh of profit each year is tax-free only for shares and funds that buy shares (section 198). It does not apply to silver.

6. Five Checks Before You Buy or Sell Silver

Five checks, about ten minutes.

  1. Work out the extra on a coin. Take the day's silver rate on ibjarates.com. It is for 1 kg, before GST. For example, on 23 November it was Rs. 1,52,625 a kg. That is about Rs. 152.6 a gram, so the silver in a 10 g coin was worth about Rs. 1,526. With 3% GST, that is about Rs. 1,572. Compare it with the coin's price.
  2. Ask the buyback price before you buy. Ask the shop or app what it pays back today for the same coin, and whether GST and making charges come back. They usually do not.
  3. Compare an ETF's price with its iNAV before you buy. The iNAV is on the NSE website's ETF page and the fund company's site. If the price is more than about Rs. 1 above the iNAV on every Rs. 100, you are paying for silver you do not get.
  4. Compare the fee and how much it trades. The fee is in the fund's monthly report, called a factsheet, on its website. How much it trades is shown on the NSE page and in your app as "volume".
  5. Count the months before you sell. The lower tax comes after 12 months for an ETF and 24 months for coins or a FoF. Each sale also has a fixed fee.

For a wedding or a gift, a coin is the silver itself, and its extra price is part of the gift. For saving, the ETF had the lowest costs we measured. Without a demat account, or for a monthly SIP, a FoF's direct plan works without a broker. If you already hold coins, ask two shops for their buyback price before you sell.

Key Takeaways

  • Check a coin's price against the day's silver rate before paying.
  • Ask what a seller will pay back before you buy, not when you sell.
  • On an ETF, the price on the app can cost more than the fee when silver is short.
  • A silver ETF is silver you own but cannot take home or borrow against.

Frequently Asked Questions

Should I buy silver ETF now?

We do not say when to buy. But you can check if the price is close to the real value. For example, on 20 November 2026, the big silver ETFs closed within Rs. 1 of their real value on every Rs. 100 (NSE prices and AMFI NAV). What happened to money that came in near the top is in section 3.

What returns has silver ETF given?

It depends on when you started; section 3 gives one-year and three-year results. We do not give forecasts.

Which silver ETF is best?

The silver ETFs we checked gave almost the same return in the year to 19 November 2026, according to AMFI, the fund companies' association. They differ in two things: the yearly fee and how easily they trade (see section 1). Nippon India Silver ETF trades the most (see "Can I sell silver ETF anytime?").

Is silver ETF better than gold ETF?

We do not say which to buy. The data shows that silver's price goes up and down more. On a normal day, Nippon India's silver ETF moved about twice as much as its gold ETF. Let's take 29 January 2026 as an example. Both were at their highest price that day. If you bought both that day, by 20 November 2026 you had lost 59% on the silver ETF and 29% on the gold ETF (AMFI NAV history, our calculation). Silver ETFs charge 0.35% to 0.60% a year; Nippon India's gold ETF, the biggest, charges 0.81% (AMFI fee list).

Silver ETF or digital silver, which is safer?

Digital silver is silver you buy on an app. No regulator watches these apps. SEBI warned about digital gold, which apps sell the same way. A silver ETF is regulated by SEBI, and auditors, outside checkers, check its silver every six months. In our example, you got back Rs. 7 to Rs. 9 less on every Rs. 100 when you sold digital silver back the same day (see sections 1 and 4).

Where can I see silver ETF price, NAV and iNAV today?

You see the price on your share-buying app and on NSE's website. NSE's ETF page also shows the iNAV, the live value of one unit, and the fund company's website shows the NAV, the value of one unit, set once a day. If the price is more than about Rs. 1 above the iNAV on every Rs. 100, you are paying for silver you do not get (see section 2).

Can I take a loan on silver ETF units, or pledge them?

Not from a bank or a loan company (NBFC). RBI's rules allow loans on silver jewellery and coins, but not on units of a silver ETF. Some broker apps let you use units as a deposit for trading, but that is not a loan (see section 4).

Can I sell silver ETF anytime?

Yes, during market hours, if someone is buying. On a normal day the big ETFs trade a lot: Nippon India's traded Rs. 2,737 crore on 20 November 2026. Some small ones traded under Rs. 3 crore. On a rush day, prices can stop at the day's limit, the most a price may move in one day. For example, 1 February 2026 was a special Sunday trading day for the Budget. That day, silver ETFs fell to the 20% lower limit and many sellers could not sell. Since 7 September 2026, the limit starts at 6%, and the exchange can widen it 3% at a time during the day.

How do I buy silver ETF, and can I do a SIP?

You buy it on a share-buying app, like Zerodha or Groww, with a demat account, where your units are kept. Zerodha says its SIP covers ETFs; check your own app. A silver fund of funds, a mutual fund that buys the ETF for you, also takes a SIP from Rs. 100 a month, without a demat account (see section 1).

Is silver ETF halal?

We found no Indian silver ETF with its own Shariah certificate. TASIS, a Shariah advisor in Mumbai, says it keeps a list of Shariah-compliant gold and silver ETFs and funds. You can ask them or your scholar.
