1 in 4 Jewellers Don't Follow India's Gold Verification Rule

By Mayank Gola, TrendTurtles  ·  8 min read  ·  Published: October 2026

Since April 2023, every piece of gold jewellery sold in India is legally required to carry a HUID — a code meant to let any buyer verify its purity. Three years later, about 1 in 4 jewellers still don't display it, according to BIS enforcement data reported by Indian Jeweller. BIS has had to start raiding shops and filing criminal cases just to get compliance up.

If the body responsible for enforcing this can't guarantee it, the honest answer for a buyer is: check it yourself, every single time, before you hand over money or gold.

1. Is the Jeweller BIS-Registered?

Every jeweller legally allowed to sell hallmarked gold has to register with the Bureau of Indian Standards and hold a valid licence. BIS keeps the full list public — search it yourself on the BIS list of licensed jewellers before you walk into a shop. A seller who isn't on it has no legal standing to sell hallmarked gold at all — whatever they tell you about purity, there's no regulator backing it.

2. Does the Gold Have a Verifiable HUID?

HUID is a 6-character code engraved on the piece itself, linking it to BIS's own database — the purity grade, the registered jeweller, the date it was tested. It's free to check on the BIS Care app, and it takes under a minute. If the app returns "Record Not Found," you've found one of the 1-in-4 jewellers this piece opened with — a hallmark can be stamped onto anything, but a fake HUID can't pass a database lookup. Walk away.

3. Will They Show You Documentation Without Hesitation?

A genuine seller can produce certification for a piece immediately, without being asked twice. Reluctance, vague answers about "it's somewhere in the back," or a sudden change of subject is one of the clearest signs something's being hidden — legitimate sellers have no reason to stall.

4. Is the Pricing Broken Down, Line by Line?

Ask to see the gold value, the making charge, and the GST as three separate lines — not one "all-inclusive" number you're asked to just trust. The making charge alone usually runs 12-25% of the gold's value, decided by the shop, not the government, and it's the single biggest hidden cost in most gold bills. We've broken down the full math, including a real consumer-court case, here: Gold Making Charges in India: What They Really Cost You.

5. Are They Pushing You to Decide Right Now?

"Today only," "last piece left," "price is going up tomorrow" — these are pressure tactics, not facts about gold. Scammers specifically create a sense of urgency to stop you doing proper research before you pay. A legitimate jeweller isn't going anywhere tomorrow, and neither is a fair price. If you feel rushed, that's the moment to slow down on purpose.

21,000 customers paid ₹2,000 a month into a Chennai jeweller's gold-savings scheme. When it collapsed, the company admitted it owed them more than ₹75 crore in gold they had already paid for.

The jeweller was Nathella Sampathu Chetty — a 77-year-old business that shut seven stores and was booked under criminal breach of trust and cheating charges once the scheme fell apart. That's what this problem looks like at its worst — paying in advance, over months, for gold you don't hold yet, with no way to verify the jeweller will still be there when the scheme matures. It's the sharpest version of the same question this whole piece is about: before you commit money or gold to someone, can you actually verify they'll do what they said?

Key Takeaways

  • About 1 in 4 Indian jewellers still don't comply with the mandatory HUID display rule — regulatory enforcement alone isn't enough, you have to verify yourself.
  • Check five things before you buy, exchange, or save with any jeweller: BIS registration, a verifiable HUID, willingness to show documentation, itemized pricing, and no pressure tactics.
  • "Record Not Found" on the BIS Care app means walk away — a fake hallmark can be stamped on, a fake HUID can't pass the database.
  • Gold-savings schemes carry the highest risk, since you pay before you receive anything — one Chennai jeweller owed 21,000 customers ₹75 crore when his scheme collapsed.
  • Making charges, the one cost that's almost always under-explained, usually run 12-25% of the gold value — the full math is in a separate piece, linked above.
