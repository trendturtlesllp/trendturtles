Buyers paid ₹396.30 for one unit of a Nasdaq ETF with a real value of ₹118.14 on 18 September 2026, 3.35 times its value.

The Motilal Oswal Nasdaq Q50 ETF, MONQ50 on the exchange, is a fund you buy and sell like a share. In two weeks its price rose 179%, but its real value stayed about the same, and more units were traded as the price rose. Before you buy any ETF, check its real value as well as its price.

On 4 September the same ETF cost ₹141.93 and held about the same value of shares. People kept buying as the price rose. If you put in ₹1 lakh at the end of 18 September, you had about ₹54,950 on 30 October. That is 45% less. If you bought on 30 October, you still paid 85% above the value. You can check this gap, ETF price vs NAV, before you buy. NAV is the real value of 1 unit.

ETF Price vs NAV: An ETF Has Two Prices

An ETF (exchange traded fund) is a fund you buy and sell on the stock exchange, like a share. It holds many shares, or gold or silver. Each piece of the fund is called a unit. MONQ50 follows the Nasdaq Q-50 index, a list of shares traded in the US, so it has to buy foreign shares.

The first price is the NAV (net asset value), the real value of 1 unit. It is announced at the end of the day. The iNAV (indicative NAV) is an estimate of the same value during trading hours.

The second price is the market price, the one on your app. If it is above the NAV, the gap is called a premium. If it is below, the gap is called a discount.

Firms called market makers usually keep the two close. A fund house is the company that runs a fund. A market maker gets new units from it and sells them on the exchange. These extra units bring a high price down. If new units cannot be made, the price can move away from the value.

Why New Units Are Hard to Make

The reason is a legal limit on buying foreign shares. A mutual fund collects money from many people and invests it. An ETF in India is a kind of mutual fund, and AMFI lists MONQ50 among them. All mutual funds in India together have an overseas investing limit of US$7 billion. The head of research at the financial firm Bajaj Capital told Mint, a business newspaper, what this means: "additional units of an ETF cannot be created even if there is demand." In simple words: when the limit is used up, no new units can be made, however many people want to buy.

Motilal Oswal warned about this in December 2024, for MONQ50 and MON100, its Nasdaq 100 ETF. The fund house asked investors to check iNAV and use limit orders, according to INDmoney, an investing app. A limit order sets the highest price you will pay.

Why the Gap Grew After 7 September 2026

An ETF's daily price limit is the highest and lowest price it can trade at that day. Until September 2026, that was 20% above or below its NAV of two days earlier. By early September, MONQ50's price was at this limit. On 4 September that was ₹141.93, which is 20% above its 2 September NAV of ₹118.28.

SEBI, the stock market regulator, then changed where this limit is counted from. It now starts from the ETF's last closing price, not from its NAV. NSE applied this from 7 September 2026.

In simple words: each day's limit now starts from yesterday's price, even if that price is already above the value. So the price of an ETF that holds shares can rise by up to 20% every day, even if the NAV does not move. Gold and silver ETFs start with a 6% daily limit. It can be widened in steps during the day, and there is no maximum. Mirae Asset, another fund house, told investors that a premium "can also go beyond the earlier 20% level", according to Cafemutual, a mutual fund news site. In simple words: the premium can now go higher than 20%.

SEBI wants the limit to start from the NAV again from 1 April 2027. Exchanges and fund houses must first solve the practical problems of making this change.

On 4 September the price could not move past its limit, and there were only 95 trades, all at ₹141.93. On 18 September there were 24,329 trades. The same units can be bought and sold many times in a day. A better count is the units buyers kept in their accounts. Buyers kept 17,832 on 4 September and 6,30,997 on 18 September. That is about 35 times as many. The NAV did not rise.

On 21 September the price reached ₹471.99 and ended the day at ₹317.04. The highest number of units traded in one day came on 24 September, after this fall.

We cannot know why each person bought. No source says who the buyers were. Every buyer bought from a seller, so some people also sold at these high prices. When a price keeps rising fast, look at the value.

The premium fell from 235.5% to 68.3% in three trading days. At the close of trading, it then went down to 56.4% on 29 September and back up to 111.8% on 14 October. A director at the wealth firm Anand Rathi Wealth told Mint the September fall was "driven by the unwinding of this premium, rather than a collapse in the underlying US stocks". In simple words: the price fell because the extra paid above the value got smaller, not because the shares fell.

Silver ETFs, January 2026: the price stayed lower after the value recovered

Silver ETFs also traded above their value, and that gap went away quickly. Silver ETFs had risen a lot over one year. IIFL, a stock broker, reported that small and medium investors then bought them in large numbers. We checked seven, including the four largest. On 21 January 2026 they closed 3% to 7% above their NAV. Not every silver ETF was this far above its NAV. The NAV and the closing price are set at different times of day, so part of this gap may be only that time difference. On 22 January they closed 10% to 16% lower, while their NAVs fell 6% to 7%. All seven ended that day below NAV, and they were still below it on 23 January. These numbers come from NSE's price files for 21 January and 22 January.

Nippon India's silver ETF, SILVERBEES, shows what this meant. By 23 January its NAV was back above the 21 January level, at ₹299.75 against ₹298.67. Its price was still 5.8% lower, at ₹292.73 against ₹310.68. If you bought at the end of 21 January, you paid above the NAV, and two days later your units were priced below it. Sources: NSE's price file and AMFI's NAV history.

Mirae Asset NYSE FANG+ ETF, 2024: a 26.3% premium, then a small loss

This ETF holds big US technology companies. In April 2024 it traded at a 26.3% premium. If you put in ₹1 lakh then, you had about ₹97,200 by the end of June. 5paisa, a stock broker, reported this, citing Value Research, a fund research firm. The price had come down close to the NAV. Your loss was small only because the fund's value rose about 21% in those months, by our calculation. Its NAV went from ₹76.91 on 19 April to ₹93.43 on 28 June, according to AMFI.

What a Premium Costs You

The premium is money you pay above the value of the shares. At ₹217.76 for ₹117.63 of shares, ₹100.13 of the price is premium. That is 46% of what you pay. If the premium goes to zero and the shares do not move, you lose that 46%.

Many people have paid some premium. BusinessLine, a business newspaper, looked at six international ETFs so far in 2026. It estimates that ₹3,094 crore of the ₹10,440 crore traded was at prices above NAV. That is about ₹30 of every ₹100 traded. We found no reported case of a named person who lost money this way.

On the public forum of Zerodha, a stock broker, we found people who could not understand the gap. In May 2025, one user asked why an ETF was down 20% from its high when the Nasdaq 100 index it follows was down only 4.4%. Another could not find the iNAV on the fund house's website.

You can also pay a premium without buying the ETF yourself. A fund of funds is a fund that invests in other funds. Some hold these ETFs and value them at the exchange price, 5paisa reports, so their NAV includes the premium.

How to Check an ETF's Premium

These six steps work for any ETF, and the first three matter most.

  1. Compare the price rise with the NAV rise. If the price has risen fast, ask if the NAV rose with it. Between 4 and 18 September, MONQ50's price rose 179% and its NAV fell 0.5%.
  2. Find the iNAV before you order. Look on the fund house's ETF page or the app you buy on. For MONQ50 and MON100, Motilal Oswal says the iNAV is calculated "considering only currency movement from the previous day's closing NAV". In simple words: for these two, the iNAV starts from the last NAV and changes only when the rupee moves against the dollar. It does not follow that day's share prices, so use it only as an estimate.
  3. Work out two numbers. For example, say the price is ₹120 and the iNAV is ₹100. Subtract the iNAV from the price, and ₹20 is left. ₹20 divided by ₹100 gives the premium, which is 20%. ₹20 divided by ₹120 gives the part of your price you can lose, which is 16.7%.
  4. Decide if you accept losing that part of your money. The fund's shares can also fall. Mirae Asset tells buyers of its overseas ETFs to compare price and iNAV and avoid large premiums. No rule says what "large" means, so the decision is yours.
  5. Use a limit order. A market order buys at whatever price sellers ask at that moment, so you can pay more than you planned. A limit order stops you paying above the price you set. Varsity, Zerodha's learning site, says to always use a limit order after checking the iNAV.
  6. Check what your fund of funds holds. Its monthly report lists what it owns. If it holds an ETF priced above its NAV, part of your fund's NAV may be that premium.

If you already hold an ETF: use step 3 to see how much of today's price is premium. We do not tell anyone to buy, hold or sell. A SEBI-registered adviser can look at what you hold and what you paid.

If you are buying your first ETF on an app: the price on the screen is not the value of the fund, so do steps 1 to 3 first.

If you invest a fixed amount every month: check the premium before you set up the order, and again every month.

If you saw a big rise in an ETF and want to buy: start with step 1, before you look at anything else.

Key Takeaways

  • When an ETF's price rises fast, check if its NAV rose too.
  • A premium is money above the value of the shares. If it shrinks, you lose money even when the shares do not fall.
  • When new units are hard to make, a premium can stay large or fall suddenly.
  • Since 7 September 2026, an ETF's daily price limit starts from its last closing price. So the premium can grow every day.
  • For any ETF, check the iNAV before you order and work out what part of the price is premium.
