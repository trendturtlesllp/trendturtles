Index funds charge higher fees than ETFs. Tata's Nifty 50 index fund charges 0.26% a year and its Nifty 50 ETF, 0.09%.

In 19 of 20 fund companies that run both an index fund and an ETF, the index fund charges more, but the ETF adds a broker's charges, and its price is dependent on market fluctuations.

Source: the yearly fee list of AMFI, the group of fund companies, November 2026; NSE's daily price file for 13 to 18 November 2026 and AMFI's real value list for the same days.

By Mayank Gola, TrendTurtles  ·  9 min read  ·  Published: November 2026

ETF or index fund, which is better? Tata Mutual Fund runs both on the Nifty 50, the list of India's 50 biggest companies on NSE, the National Stock Exchange. Its ETF charges the lower fee, but is it cheaper once the broker and the price are added?

This is the main article of our series on what you really buy in an ETF. It adds up every cost on both sides, with Tata's two funds as the example. The costs start with how you buy each one.

1. Same Shares, Two Ways to Buy

An index fund is a mutual fund that buys only the shares of an index, such as the Nifty 50. You buy its units, the small equal pieces a fund is divided into, from the fund company, on its website or a fund app. You pay the NAV, the real value of one unit, worked out once a day after the market closes. If your money reaches the fund by 3 pm, you get that day's NAV. When you sell, the fund company pays you the NAV too.

An ETF, or exchange traded fund, is also a mutual fund that buys the same index shares. But its units are listed on the stock exchange, so you buy and sell them on your stock app, like a share. For this you need a broker, the firm that runs the app, and a demat account, where the broker keeps your units and shares. The ETF price is whatever buyers and sellers agree on during the day, so it can be above or below the NAV.

The exchange shows the iNAV, the live value of one unit, at most 15 seconds behind the market, so you can compare it with the price. What does each fund cost you every year?

2. The Yearly Fee: Index Funds Charge More

Every fund takes a yearly fee from your money, called the expense ratio. It is cut from the fund's value every day, so you never see a bill. An index fund has two plans. The direct plan is bought straight from the fund company, and the word "Direct" is in its name. The regular plan is bought through an agent, who is paid from the fee. For every ₹100 you hold, Tata's direct plan takes 26 paise a year and its ETF takes 9 paise. Here is the fee for the Nifty 50 funds of eight fund companies:

3. The Other Costs: Buying, Selling and Holding

An index fund's direct plan has no broker, so most of these costs do not apply. An ETF is bought through a broker, so they do. Each broker sets its own charges; the ETF column uses Zerodha's published charges as one example.

The DP charge is a fixed amount for each sale, not a percentage. If you sell 1 unit of Tata's ETF at ₹273.38, ₹15.34 is 5.6% of it. The more you sell in one go, the smaller that share becomes. If you sell in parts on different days, you pay it each time.

One cost is not written in any list: the price.

4. The Price You Get: Below or Above the Real Value

On 16 November, Tata's ETF closed at ₹273.38 for a unit worth ₹275.90. So a seller at the closing price got ₹2.52 less per unit, 0.91% below the value. Only 187 trades happened in it that day. On other days that week the gap was smaller: 0.46% on 13 November, 0.50% on 17 November and 0.06% on 18 November.

Put that next to the fee. The ETF saves 0.17% a year against Tata's direct plan (0.26% minus 0.09%). One sale at 0.91% below the value takes away about 5 years of that saving. One sale at 0.06% below takes away about 4 months of it.

A busy ETF stays closer to its value. Over the same four days, Nippon India ETF Nifty 50 BeES had 43,026 to 70,040 trades a day and closed 0.02% to 0.36% below its value. Tata's ETF, with 93 to 187 trades, closed 0.06% to 0.91% below. Our gold ETF article shows why the number of trades matters. In 2024, an investor wrote on a Zerodha forum that a market order, an order at whatever price is on offer, in SBI's Nifty Next 50 ETF did not go through, because too few people were trading it.

The price can also be above the value, and then the buyer pays extra. A Value Research study (March 2026) found that ETFs of foreign shares were more than 5% away from their value on about 45% of days, while ETFs of India's biggest companies rarely were. On the Nasdaq 100, a list of US companies, a fund that holds the Indian ETF showed about 53% for one year, while four other funds on the same list showed about 30%. The difference came from the ETF's price being above its value, Value Research reported in September 2026. Our ETF price article explains how buyers lose when that gap closes.

So how do the two compare, cost by cost?

Both are taxed alike. For funds that hold Indian shares, if you sell within 12 months, the gain is taxed at 20%. After 12 months, it is taxed at 12.5% on gains above ₹1.25 lakh a year.

ETFs have one safety rule that index funds do not need. If an ETF closes more than 1% below its value for 7 trading days in a row, or has no buyers or sellers for 3 trading days, you can sell it back to the fund company at the NAV, with no exit load.

So how do you get in and out of each at the lowest cost?

6. The Cheaper Way In and Out

  1. Compare the yearly fee. Open AMFI's fee page, pick the fund company and the fund, and read the fee for the direct plan, the regular plan and the ETF.
  2. For an index fund, check the plan name and the exit load. Make sure the word "Direct" is in the fund's name before you pay. On the fund's page, read how many days the exit load applies, and do not sell before then.
  3. For an ETF, check the price against the iNAV before you buy or sell. Open NSE's ETF page, find the ETF, and compare its price with its iNAV. The page also shows how many units traded that day.
  4. Use a limit order for an ETF. A limit order is a price you set on your stock app; the trade happens only at that price or better. Set it close to the iNAV.
  5. Count your broker's charges. On your broker's charges page, find the brokerage, the DP charge for each sale and the yearly demat fee. Divide the DP charge by the amount you plan to sell, to see its share.
  6. If your ETF stays below its value for many days, ask the fund company to buy your units back at the NAV, under the rule in section 5.

If you invest a small amount every month: do steps 1, 2 and 5, because a fixed charge is a bigger share of a small amount.

If you invest a large amount and hold it for years: do steps 1, 3 and 4, because the yearly fee adds up over time and one trade's price gap does not.

If you have no demat account: an index fund needs none; for an ETF, add the demat fee in step 5.

Key Takeaways

  • One sale below the value can cancel years of an ETF's fee saving.
  • An index fund always pays the NAV, but check the plan and the exit load.
  • Check the fee, the iNAV and your broker's charges before you choose.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is an ETF a mutual fund?

Yes. An ETF is a mutual fund whose units are listed on the stock exchange (see section 1).

Are an ETF and an index fund on the same index the same?

They hold the same shares, but you buy them differently and pay different costs (see section 5).

Does an ETF or an index fund follow the index more closely?

Both must stay close to it. Tracking error is how far a fund's daily return moves away from the index over a year, and SEBI caps it at 2% for both. AMFI shows each fund's figure on its tracking error page.
