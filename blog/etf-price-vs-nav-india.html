ETF's stock market price is not its real value. Buyers paid ₹396.30 for one unit worth ₹118.14.

In the two weeks, Motilal Oswal Nasdaq Q50 ETF's price rose 179%, but its real value remained almost the same. Before you buy any ETF, check its real value as well as its price.

By Mayank Gola, TrendTurtles  ·  13 min read  ·  Published: November 2026

On 18 September 2026, one unit of the Motilal Oswal Nasdaq Q50 ETF ended the day at ₹396.30 on the stock market. The real value of that unit was only ₹118.14. Two weeks earlier, the same unit cost ₹141.93, and its real value was ₹118.74. NAV (net asset value) means the real value of 1 unit. This article explains why ETF prices are different from NAV.

How can a price go so far above the real value? To see this, you first need to know that an ETF has a price and a real value.

1. Why ETF Prices Are Different From NAV: A Price and a Real Value

ETF stands for exchange traded fund. A fund collects money from many people and buys things like shares, gold or silver. A share is a small part of a company. An ETF is sold in small pieces called units, and you buy these units on the stock market through an app.

Each unit has a real value. The fund adds up what it owns, takes away what it owes, and divides this by the number of units. For the Nasdaq ETF on 18 September, this came to ₹118.14 for 1 unit.

Each unit also has a price. This is the price you see on your app. Buyers and sellers decide this price. When many people want to buy, the price goes up.

So you can pay more than the real value. On 18 September, buyers paid ₹396.30 for a unit worth ₹118.14. The extra ₹278.16 is called the premium. Here the premium is 235.5%, because they paid 235.5% more than the real value. The extra ₹278.16 gets them nothing more. They still own something worth ₹118.14.

Most of the time, an ETF's premium stays small. The next section shows why.

2. Why the Price Usually Stays Close to the Real Value

When too many people want to buy an ETF, its price goes up. To bring the price down, more units must come up for sale. Big firms called market makers bring these extra units. Here is how they do it.

Say the real value is ₹118.14 and the price goes above it. A market maker gets new units from the fund house, the company that runs the fund, at about ₹118.14. You cannot do this on your app, because new units are made only in very large lots. For the Nasdaq ETF in this article, one lot is 6,00,000 units. The market maker sells these units on the stock market at the higher price and keeps the difference. Now more units are for sale, so buyers do not need to pay extra. The price comes down towards ₹118.14.

This works only if new units can be made.

Now come back to the Nasdaq ETF from the start of this article. It buys shares of US companies. To make new units, the fund must buy more of these shares. So it must send money to the US, and India limits this money. Value Research, a fund research firm, says no new units of ETFs like this one have been made since April 2024. So no new units came in, and the price depended only on how many people wanted to buy and sell. The stock market also has a limit on how far a price can move in one day, and on 7 September 2026 this limit changed. The next section shows how the limit worked and what changed.

3. Why the Premium Grew After 7 September 2026

Under this daily price limit, the price cannot go above the top limit or below the bottom limit on any day.

Until 6 September 2026, this limit came from the NAV. The price could move only 20% above or below the NAV of two days earlier. This stopped the premium from going far above 20%. From April 2026, this ETF's price had reached this limit, so it could not go higher. On 4 September, its top limit was ₹141.93, 20% above the 2 September NAV.

Then SEBI, the body that makes rules for the stock market, changed the rule. The new rule started on 7 September 2026. For ETFs that buy shares, like this one, the limit is still up to 20%. But it now comes from yesterday's closing price, not from the NAV. The closing price is the average price of the last 30 minutes of the day.

Here is what that means. Say the closing price is ₹141.93 and the real value is about ₹118. The next day the price can go up to about ₹170. If it closes there, the limit for the day after is about ₹204. So the price can rise by up to 20% every day, even if the real value does not change.

This is what happened to this ETF. Its name on the stock market is MONQ50.

Did people really buy at these high prices? Yes. On 18 September, 22,29,451 units were bought, and buyers kept 6,30,997 of them at the end of the day. We do not know why they paid this much. What happened to their money?

4. What a Premium Means for Your Money

To get your money out, you usually must sell your units to another buyer on the stock market. Until the real value reaches what you paid, you can make a profit only if this next buyer also pays extra.

After 18 September, the extra began to disappear. By 23 September the price was ₹202.92. Mint, a business newspaper, spoke to a director at Anand Rathi Wealth, a financial firm. The director said the fall was "driven by the unwinding of this premium, rather than a collapse in the underlying US stocks". In simple words: the value of the US shares inside the ETF remained almost the same. The price fell because buyers stopped paying so much extra.

You can lose money even when the real value rises. On 7 October, the real value was ₹123.09, more than the ₹118.14 of 18 September. But the price was ₹258.57. A buyer who paid ₹396.30 on 18 September and sold on 7 October lost ₹137.73 on each unit.

On 30 October, the price was ₹217.76 and the real value was ₹117.63. If you put ₹1 lakh into this ETF on 18 September, your money was worth about ₹54,950. You lost 45%, while the real value remained almost the same.

This ETF is not the only one. Other ETFs have also had a premium that later became smaller. The reasons were not always the same.

5. Other ETFs Have Had a Premium Before

Why Silver ETF Prices Fell More Than Their NAV, January 2026

IIFL, a company that buys and sells shares for people, reported that silver ETFs had gone up a lot in one year, and then many people with small and medium amounts of money rushed to buy them. At the end of the day on 21 January 2026, the prices of seven silver ETFs were 3% to 7% above their NAV. The 3% to 7% is not exact. The closing price and the NAV may use silver prices from two different times of the day. The next day, silver fell and their NAVs fell 6% to 7%, but their prices fell 10% to 16%. Buyers lost money because silver fell, and they also lost the extra money they had paid. In the next case, buyers lost money even though the NAV rose.

Mirae Asset NYSE FANG+ ETF, 2024

In January 2024, Mirae Asset stopped taking money from large investors for new units in its ETFs that buy foreign shares. Market makers could still get them until March. After that no new units came in. On 19 April 2024, the price was 26.3% above the NAV. 5paisa, a share trading company, reported figures from Value Research: ₹1 lakh put in then was worth about ₹97,200 by the end of June. The NAV rose about 21%, from ₹76.91 to ₹93.43, but almost all of the extra money went away. So the buyer lost about ₹2,800. We now have three cases. Here they are together.

But a premium can come back. On 30 October 2026, the FANG+ ETF's price was ₹228.59 and its NAV was ₹164.13. That is about 39% above its NAV.

The reason can change, but the check is the same. Here is what to do before you buy any ETF.

6. How to Check an ETF Before You Buy

  1. Check if the price has gone up much faster than the NAV lately. The NAV is on the fund house's website.
  2. Find the iNAV. The fund announces the NAV only at the end of the day, but you buy during the day. So during the day you look at the iNAV, an estimate of the NAV. Look on the fund house's website, or on your app if it shows the iNAV. For some ETFs this number is very rough. The questions at the end of this article explain why.
  3. Calculate the extra money. Say the price is ₹396.30 and the iNAV is ₹118.14. The extra money is ₹278.16. Divide ₹278.16 by the iNAV and multiply by 100. You get a 235.5% premium. Now divide ₹278.16 by the price, ₹396.30, and multiply by 100. You get 70.2%. This second number is the part of your money you can lose if the premium goes away.
  4. Use a limit order. When you buy, the app asks how you want to buy. A market order buys at the price sellers ask now. A limit order lets you set the highest price you will pay. Say you set ₹120 and sellers ask ₹396.30. Your order will not buy at ₹396.30. It will buy only if the price comes down to ₹120 or less. Varsity, the learning site of Zerodha, says to check the iNAV and always use a limit order.
  5. Decide if you accept the risk. The shares in the fund can also fall, so you can lose more than the extra money you paid. No rule says how much premium is too much, so you must decide.

If you already own an ETF: use step 3 to see how much of today's price is extra money. We do not tell anyone to buy, keep or sell. An adviser registered with SEBI can look at what you own.

If you are buying your first ETF: the price on the screen is not the real value. Do steps 1 to 3 first.

If you put in money every month: check the premium before you start, and then check it every month, because it can change.

Key Takeaways

  • The premium is the extra money you pay above the NAV.
  • You get your money out by selling to the next buyer, so you can lose money even when the real value rises.
  • Since 7 September 2026, the daily price limit for ETFs that buy shares comes from yesterday's closing price, so the premium can grow every day.
  • Before you buy any ETF, check the iNAV and calculate how much extra you are paying.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why are ETF prices different from the NAV?

ETF prices are different from the NAV because buyers and sellers decide the price on the stock market, but the NAV comes from the value of what the fund owns (see section 1).

What is NAV in an ETF?

NAV stands for net asset value. It is the real value of 1 unit of the fund, and the fund announces it at the end of the day (see section 1).

What is iNAV?

iNAV stands for indicative net asset value. It is an estimate of the NAV during the day (see section 6, step 2). For MONQ50 and MON100, two Motilal Oswal ETFs that buy US shares, the iNAV is calculated "considering only currency movement from the previous day's closing NAV". In simple words: their iNAV changes only when the price of 1 US dollar in rupees changes. It does not follow the US shares inside the fund during the day, so it is only a rough number.

Why are gold ETF prices different from the NAV?

Gold ETF prices are different from the NAV for the same reason as other ETFs (see section 1). On 21 January 2026, Nippon India's gold ETF, GOLDBEES, ended the day at ₹134.97, about 6% above its NAV of ₹127.02. The next day it ended at ₹124.34, just below its NAV of ₹124.57.

Why can't new units of MONQ50 be made?

New units of MONQ50 cannot be made because the fund must send money to the US to buy more shares, and India limits this money (see section 2). An ETF is one kind of mutual fund, and all mutual funds in India together can send only 7 billion US dollars, or 700 crore US dollars. India also has a separate limit of 1 billion US dollars for ETFs like this one. Reports differ on which limit stopped MONQ50.

Is the daily price limit the same for every ETF?

No. For ETFs that buy shares, the limit is up to 20% (see section 3). Gold and silver ETFs start with a 6% limit, which can be increased in steps during the day.

Will the price limit change again?

SEBI wants the limit to come from the NAV again, but from yesterday's NAV, not the NAV of two days earlier. SEBI wants this to start on 1 April 2027. First, stock exchanges and fund companies must solve some practical problems.

Can a fund of funds also have a premium?

Yes. A fund of funds is a fund that buys other funds, such as ETFs. 5paisa reports that some of these funds use an ETF's price, not its NAV, when they add up what they own. So part of your fund's value may be premium. Read the fund's monthly report to see what it owns.
