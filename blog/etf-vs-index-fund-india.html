Index funds charge higher fees than ETFs. Tata's Nifty 50 index fund charges 0.26% a year and its Nifty 50 ETF, 0.09%.

In 19 of 20 fund companies that run both an index fund and an ETF, the index fund charges more, but the ETF adds a broker's charges, and its price is dependent on market fluctuations.

Source: the yearly fee list of AMFI, the group of fund companies, November 2026.

By Mayank Gola, TrendTurtles  ·  7 min read  ·  Published: November 2026

ETF or index fund, which is better? Tata Mutual Fund runs both on the Nifty 50, the list of India's 50 biggest companies on NSE, the National Stock Exchange. Its ETF charges the lower fee, but is it cheaper once the broker and the price are added?

This article lists every cost of both, using Tata's two funds. The costs start with how you buy each one.

1. Same Shares, Two Ways to Buy

An index fund is a mutual fund that buys only the shares of an index, such as the Nifty 50. You buy its units, the small equal pieces a fund is divided into, from the fund company, on its website or a fund app. You pay the NAV, the real value of one unit, worked out once a day after the market closes. When you sell, the fund company pays you the NAV. You do not need a demat account.

An ETF, or exchange traded fund, is also a mutual fund that buys the same index shares. But you buy and sell it on your stock app, like a share. For this you need a broker, the firm that runs the app, and a demat account, where the broker keeps your units.

What does each one cost you every year?

2. The Yearly Fee: Index Funds Charge More

Every fund takes a yearly fee from your money, called the expense ratio. It is cut from the fund's value every day, so you never see a bill. An index fund has two plans. The direct plan is bought straight from the fund company, and the word "Direct" is in its name. The regular plan is bought through an agent, who is paid from the fee, so it costs more.

3. The Other Costs: Buying, Selling and Holding

An ETF is bought through a broker, so it has a broker's charges. Each broker sets its own. The ETF column below uses the published charges of Zerodha, a broker, as one example. An index fund's direct plan has no broker, so it has no broker's charges.

When you sell, both also pay a very small tax, ₹1 on every ₹1 lakh (ETF, index fund). An ETF also pays tiny fees to the stock exchange on each trade, plus GST on the broker's charges. Tata's ETF has no exit fee.

The ₹15.34 is the same whether you sell a small amount or a large one. So on a small sale it is a bigger part of your money. These costs are all written in a list. One cost is not: the price.

4. The Price: An ETF Can Sell Below Its Real Value

When you sell an ETF, you get what a buyer pays. On 16 November 2026, Tata's ETF ended the day at ₹273.38, while one unit was worth ₹275.90. A seller at that price got ₹2.52 less per unit than its value. On 18 November, the gap was only 16 paise.

The gap is usually smaller in an ETF that many people trade. On 16 November, 187 trades happened in Tata's ETF and over 70,000 in Nippon India's Nifty 50 ETF. In 2024, an investor wrote on a Zerodha forum that an order in SBI's Nifty Next 50 ETF did not go through, because too few people were trading it. So the ETF's lower yearly fee can be lost to its other costs and its price, most of all on small amounts and in ETFs that few people trade. How do you get in and out of each at the lowest cost?

6. The Cheaper Way In and Out

  1. Compare the yearly fee. Open AMFI's fee page, pick the fund company and the fund, and read the fee for the direct plan, the regular plan and the ETF.
  2. For an index fund, buy the direct plan and wait out the exit fee. Check that "Direct" is in the fund's name before you pay. On the fund's page, read how many days the exit fee applies, and do not sell before then.
  3. For an ETF, check the price against its live value before you buy or sell. The live value, worked out again and again during the day, is called the iNAV. Open NSE's ETF page, find the ETF, and compare its price with its iNAV. The page also shows how many units were traded that day.
  4. Set your own price for an ETF. On your stock app, choose a limit order: you type the price, and the trade happens only at that price or a better one (lower when you buy, higher when you sell). Type a price close to the iNAV.
  5. Check your broker's charges. On your broker's charges page, find the fee to buy, the fee for each sale and the yearly demat fee. Sell in one go rather than in small parts, as each sale pays the fixed fee again.

If you invest a small amount every month: do steps 1, 2 and 5, because a fixed fee is a bigger part of a small amount.

If you invest a large amount and hold it for years: do steps 1, 3 and 4, because the yearly fee adds up over the years.

If you have no demat account: an index fund needs none; an ETF adds step 5's demat fee.

Key Takeaways

  • An ETF's lower fee comes with broker and demat charges.
  • An ETF can sell below its value; an index fund always pays the NAV.
  • Check the fee, the live value and your broker's charges before you choose.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is an ETF a mutual fund?

Yes. An ETF is a mutual fund whose units are bought and sold on the stock exchange (see section 1).

Are an ETF and an index fund on the same index the same?

They hold the same shares, but you buy them differently and pay different costs (see section 5).

How are ETFs and index funds taxed?

The same way, for funds that hold Indian shares. If you sell within 12 months, the gain is taxed at 20%. After that, it is taxed at 12.5% on gains above ₹1.25 lakh a year.

Do I need a demat account for an index fund?

No. You can buy an index fund straight from the fund company; only an ETF needs a demat account (see section 1).
