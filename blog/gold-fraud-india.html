Over 1 lakh people lost ₹1,404 crore invested in a gold company, Tamil Nadu Police records show. Most gold frauds are planned before you pay.

The company was Aarudhra Gold Trading. Most gold frauds work like this: someone wins your trust first, then takes your gold or money. Here are the six common ways, with real cases and the check that stops each one.

Tamil Nadu Police records, as of June 2023, reported in November 2025. Source: Free Press Journal, a daily newspaper.

By Mayank Gola, TrendTurtles  ·  8 min read  ·  Published: October 2026

Aarudhra Gold Trading was a company in Tamil Nadu. It allegedly promised people monthly returns of 10% to 30%. By June 2023, 1,04,433 people had asked it for about ₹2,438 crore back. Police records show that ₹1,404 crore of that was never paid back, the Free Press Journal reported in November 2025.

The police unit for big money frauds, the Economic Offences Wing, has filed charges against 40 people and firms.

Aarudhra is one example of a vanishing gold scheme. It is one of six common ways gold buyers are cheated.

Gold Fraud: The Six Common Ways, With Real Cases

1. Fake hallmarks (Kanpur, 2026)

A hallmark is the government's purity stamp on gold. In October 2026, a woman came to a jewellery shop in Kanpur to sell a gold chain and two rings. The chain carried an 18-carat hallmark, which means 75% gold. A test found only about 10% gold. The shop's manager called the police, and police later seized a hallmarking machine from a shop in the city, Amar Ujala reported.

2. Fake trading apps (Jaipur, 2025)

In 2025, a gems trader in Jaipur got a WhatsApp link from someone calling herself Kritika Thakur. The link opened a website that claimed high returns on gold trading. He put in ₹5 lakh, then about ₹6 crore in instalments. When he tried to take money out, he was told that 30% would be cut as "income tax". Over several payments, he lost ₹12 crore in all, ETV Bharat reported.

3. Vanishing gold schemes (Heera Gold, 2024)

Investors in Heera Gold Exim were promised a 36% dividend, a share of profit, on the money they put in. When the company stopped paying, investors in several states complained of cheating. Bail is a court's permission to stay out of jail during a case. In October 2024, the Supreme Court cancelled the bail of the company's managing director, because she had not raised ₹580 crore to pay investors' claims. During the hearing, a judge asked: "Where is the money?"

4. Pledged gold taken or swapped (Tamil Nadu and Andhra Pradesh, alleged)

To pledge gold is to hand it over as security for a loan. In 2010, 494 farmers in Kallakurichi district, Tamil Nadu, pledged their gold with cooperative societies, the local bodies that lend to farmers. Together they pledged about 1,800 sovereigns; a sovereign is 8 grams. Many pledged it for their children's weddings, or to cover losses from crop damage. When they went to take it back, they were told it had been stolen, a farmers' union leader said. In January 2026, hundreds of farmers protested in Chennai. They said gold and money were missing from at least 75 cooperative societies, The News Minute reported. These are the farmers' allegations.

In Thoothukudi, Tamil Nadu, police are investigating an alleged ₹29 crore fraud at another cooperative society, from 2019 to 2021. Police say members' gold was taken from the society and pledged again at other banks. To hide it, the gold was pledged in the names of more than 10 people, including a car driver and domestic workers. The latest four arrests came in October 2026.

It happens inside banks too. An appraiser is the person who checks and values the gold you pledge. In 2026, police arrested the appraiser at a Bank of Baroda branch in Eluru district, Andhra Pradesh. He is accused of taking pledged gold from 176 gold loan accounts, worth about ₹10 crore. In October 2026, police in Kakinada arrested a former appraiser of the same bank. He is accused of replacing about 1,057 grams of customers' pledged gold, worth about ₹1.45 crore, with artificial ornaments.

5. Gold left with the jeweller (Gwalior, 2026, alleged)

Mansingh Gurjar's son was getting married in February. Gurjar had 640 grams of gold jewellery made by a jeweller in Gwalior. In the wedding rush, the jeweller gave it without a hallmark and said he would get it hallmarked later. After the wedding, Gurjar took the jewellery back to him. The jeweller said his system was down, kept the jewellery and gave him a receipt. Two days later, the shop was shut and the jeweller was gone, NPG News reported in June 2026.

Once Gurjar went to the police, dozens of others came forward. The jeweller and his brother are accused of taking more than 3.5 kg of gold, worth about ₹4 crore, from over 20 customers. Police arrested them on the Meerut highway.

6. Making charge and wastage billed higher than promised (Bangalore, 2018)

The making charge is the cost of making a piece, and wastage is the gold a shop says is lost while making it. A government teacher in Bangalore was promised 3–5% wastage in a jeweller's gold scheme. Her bill showed 23.5%. She paid ₹78,300 for a necklace that should have cost ₹71,000, and a consumer court ordered the jeweller to pay her back, Indian Jeweller reported.

What It Costs Families

The losses fall on families. The farmers' union says many farmers have died by suicide over the missing gold. And a loss is often found only later. In 2024, LocalCircles, a citizen survey platform, wrote that many buyers learn their gold is less pure than they paid for when they try to remake or sell it.

What To Do Before You Pay, Pledge or Invest

  1. Check the hallmark code. BIS, the Bureau of Indian Standards, runs hallmarking. Type the six-character hallmark code into its free BIS Care app before you pay. If you doubt the gold later, a BIS-recognised centre tests it for ₹200.
  2. Never pay to take your own money out. If an app or website asks for a "tax" or a fee before you can withdraw, stop and tell the police.
  3. Walk away from very high returns. Promises of very high returns are the bait. If you join a jeweller's own savings scheme, a legal explainer on LiveLaw says one that runs longer than a year could be an illegal deposit scheme. So join only one that ends within a year.
  4. When you pledge gold with a bank or finance company, know the rules. Under the RBI's 2025 rules for gold loans, the lender must return your gold within seven working days after you repay. If the delay is its fault, it must pay you ₹5,000 for each day late. If your gold is lost or found less pure, it must compensate you. Check the gold when it comes back.
  5. Do not take unhallmarked pieces on a promise to hallmark them later. If you leave gold with a jeweller for any reason, get a receipt that shows the weight. It is your proof: in Kangra, Himachal Pradesh, a customer whose jeweller denied receiving 32 grams of gold showed the receipt, and a consumer court ordered the jeweller to return the gold and pay ₹35,000.
  6. Get the making charge and wastage written on the bill before you pay. A rate said aloud cannot be proved later.
  7. If it goes wrong, complain at once. Call the National Consumer Helpline on 1915. For a fraud, go to the police. Against a jeweller or a lender, you can also go to a consumer court.

Who needs this most:

  • A family buying for a wedding: steps 1, 5 and 6.
  • Someone saving in a scheme or an app: steps 2 and 3.
  • A farmer or family pledging gold for a loan: step 4.
  • Anyone who has already lost gold: step 7.

Key Takeaways

  • The six common tricks are fake hallmarks, fake trading apps, vanishing gold schemes, pledged gold taken or swapped, gold left with the jeweller, and making charges billed higher than promised.
  • An app that asks for a "tax" before you can withdraw is a trap.
  • Very high promised returns are the bait in vanishing schemes.
  • A bank or finance company must return pledged gold within seven working days of repayment, or pay ₹5,000 a day if the delay is its fault.
  • Check every hallmark code, and get every charge and every handover in writing.
