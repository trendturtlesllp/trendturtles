The raids were by the Legal Metrology Department in November 2018. It is the government department that checks weights, measures and prices in shops. Its rule: wastage must be shown on the bill on its own line, not hidden in the gold rate.

Legal Metrology Department raids, Hyderabad, November 2018. Source: The Hans India, a daily newspaper.

This article is part of our series on how gold buyers are cheated. In this one, the shop hides or changes the gold making charges and wastage it adds on top of the gold. The first episode was on fake gold hallmarks.

What the Hyderabad Raids Found

In November 2018, nine teams of the Legal Metrology Department raided jewellery showrooms across Greater Hyderabad. The officials said: "in some cases near about 25 percent is charged more in the name of wastage", Deccan Chronicle reported. In simple words: some shops were adding about a quarter more to the bill and calling it wastage.

The officials filed 85 cases in all and seized jewellery worth more than ₹1 crore, The Hans India reported. One of the rules they checked: the price of wastage must be shown on the bill on its own, not hidden inside the gold rate.

What Gold Making Charges and Wastage Mean

A gold jewellery bill starts with the gold, priced by weight at the day's rate. On top of it comes the making charge, which is the cost of turning gold into a chain or a bangle. Some shops also add wastage, which is the gold they say is lost while making the piece. A shop may write these as a fixed amount per gram, or as a percentage of the gold value.

Two researchers at IIM Kozhikode wrote a draft research paper in 2024. It says the making charge is "highly variable and is opaque to the consumer". In simple words: it changes a lot from shop to shop, and you cannot see how the shop arrived at it. The paper also quotes a 2020 news report. It put making charges on heavy gold jewellery at 6% to 25% of the gold price.

For exports, the government does set a number. In 2024, it allowed exporters to count up to 2.5% of the gold as wastage while making plain gold jewellery, and 5% for studded jewellery. In May 2024 it tried to cut these to 0.5% and 0.75%. A day later it put the change on hold, after the jewellery industry objected, Business Standard reported. This was a rule for exporters, not a limit on what shops may charge you.

A shop's making charge and wastage are not fixed by any rule, so the shop can change them easily. That is where buyers get cheated.

Four Ways Shops Charge You More

The cases below show four ways shops charge more:

  • You are told one rate and billed another. The low rate is said out loud, and the high rate appears on the bill.
  • Wastage is hidden inside the gold rate. The bill shows no wastage line, so you cannot see what you paid for it.
  • The rate you booked is changed when you collect. You pay an advance at one gold rate, and the shop charges the newer, higher one.
  • You get less when you sell the piece back. The shop cuts extra money from the price and gives a reason, such as tax.

What Buyers Went Through

1. Promised 3–5% wastage, billed 23.5% (Bangalore, 2018)

A government teacher in Bangalore joined a jeweller's gold savings scheme, paying ₹6,000 a month. She was promised low wastage of 3–5%. When she bought her necklace, the bill showed 23.5% wastage. She paid ₹78,300 for a necklace that should have cost her ₹71,000, Indian Jeweller reported.

In 2018, a consumer court in Bangalore ruled in her favour. A consumer court is where shoppers take complaints against sellers. It ordered the jeweller to return ₹7,294 with 12% yearly interest, and to pay ₹20,000 as compensation and legal costs. It also ordered the jeweller to give her 7.130 grams of 22-carat gold, which is 91.6% pure.

2. Booked at one rate, charged the new rate (2020)

In February 2020, a buyer booked a gold piece at a jewellery shop and paid an advance. The shop gave an advance slip and promised to deliver in a month. Then the lockdown closed the shop. When it reopened in June, the jeweller charged the new, higher gold rate instead of the booked one.

The buyer complained to the National Consumer Helpline, the government's helpline for shoppers. The jeweller then paid back the difference of ₹7,925, the helpline's record shows.

3. A buyback condition stamped on the bill (Pune, 2025)

In 2017, a couple in Pune bought a diamond bangle for ₹3.59 lakh. The salesperson said they could sell it back at the market rate, minus the making charges and 10%. The bill said the same. In 2022, they tried to sell it back. The shop offered much less, because it also cut "government taxes".

The salesperson pointed to a rubber stamp on the bill with harsher terms. The couple said the stamp was not there when they signed. The shop denied the charges. On 2 January 2025, a consumer court in Pune called this an unfair trade practice, Moneylife reported.

The court ordered the shop to buy the bangle back at the market rate, cutting only the making charges and 10%. It also ordered ₹30,000 in compensation and ₹7,000 in legal costs. When Moneylife wrote about it, there was no progress. The couple had gone back to court to make the shop obey the order.

4. What a "0% making charge" offer can hide (2025, one banker's view)

In 2025, investment banker Sarthak Anuja wrote about "0% making charge" offers. He wrote that jewellers often quote ₹200 more per gram than the market rate. He also wrote that actual wastage is 2–3%, but shops often bill 5%, Business Today reported. This is one person's view, not an investigation. But it shows where to look on the bill when the making charge says zero.

She was promised 3–5% wastage and billed 23.5%. A consumer court ordered the jeweller to pay her back.

What It Costs You

You pay for nothing extra. In Bangalore, the higher wastage made the buyer pay ₹78,300 instead of ₹71,000. The necklace was the same; only the bill grew.

You never get it back. When you sell or exchange a piece, you do not get back the making charge you paid. Business Standard quoted the head of a large jewellery chain in May 2026: "Customers do not recover the original making charges paid on old jewellery." At exchange, shops also take off the weight of stones, pearls and wax. They take off more if they find the gold less pure.

Without a bill, you cannot complain. In May 2024, LocalCircles, a citizen survey platform, asked gold jewellery buyers if they took a GST bill. GST is the goods and services tax, and a GST bill is a proper tax bill. Of 11,299 answers, 8% said they did not, because they paid in cash. The Bangalore buyer could prove the 23.5% because it was on her bill.

Four Tricks and What Stops Each

The trickReal caseWhat stops it
Told one wastage rate, billed anotherBangalore, 2018The making charge and wastage written down before you pay, or on the scheme card
Wastage hidden in the gold rateHyderabad raids, 2018A bill with wastage on its own line
Booked rate changed at deliveryAdvance booking, 2020The booked gold rate written on the advance slip
A harsher buyback condition stamped on the billPune, 2025The buyback terms written on the bill when you buy, and a photo of the bill

All four are stopped by the same thing: the number written down before you pay.

What To Do Before You Pay, and When You Sell

There are six steps. You can do the first three at the counter, before you pay.

  1. Ask for the making charge and wastage in writing, before you pay. In a savings scheme, get them written on the scheme card.
  2. Ask for a bill with every part on its own line. It should show the total weight, the weight of the stones, and the weight of the gold alone. It should also show the gold rate, the making charge, the wastage and the GST.
  3. Check the GST. The government's GST guidance for jewellers says GST is "3% of the total transaction value of jewellery, whether the making charge is shown separately or not". In simple words: 3% on the whole bill. If a shop adds a separate 5% on the making charge, ask why.
  4. When you book in advance, get the gold rate written on the advance slip. Then the shop cannot charge a newer rate when you collect.
  5. Get the buyback and exchange terms on the bill when you buy, and take a photo of the bill. When you sell, check the offer against those terms. If a new condition has appeared, the photo shows it was not there.
  6. If the shop will not fix it, complain. Call the National Consumer Helpline on 1915, or write on WhatsApp to 8800001915. If that fails, you can go to a consumer court, as the buyers in Bangalore and Pune did.

GST guidance: CBEC (now CBIC), the government body that runs GST, sectoral FAQ on gems and jewellery. Source: GST Council website.

Who needs this most:

  • A family buying for a wedding. It is the biggest gold bill most families pay. Do steps 1 to 3 on every piece.
  • Someone saving in a jeweller's scheme. Step 1 is yours. The Bangalore buyer was in a scheme.
  • Someone booking gold in advance. Step 4 protects the rate you booked.
  • Someone selling or exchanging old gold. Step 5 protects what you get back.

Key Takeaways

  • In 2018, Legal Metrology officials in Hyderabad said some shops charged about 25% extra as wastage.
  • Making charges vary a lot from shop to shop, and buyers cannot see how they are set.
  • A Bangalore buyer promised 3–5% wastage was billed 23.5%. A consumer court ordered the jeweller to pay her back.
  • GST on jewellery is 3% of the whole bill, making charge included, says the government's GST guidance for jewellers.
  • Get every charge, the booked rate and the buyback terms written down before you pay, and keep the bill.
