Over 1 lakh people lost ₹1,404 crore invested in a gold company, Tamil Nadu Police records show. Most gold frauds are planned before you pay.

Fake hallmarks, fake trading apps, vanishing gold schemes and swapped pledged gold all work the same way: first you are made to trust someone, then your gold or money is taken. Here is how each one works, with a real case and the check that stops it.

Tamil Nadu Police records, as of June 2023, reported in November 2025. Source: Free Press Journal, a daily newspaper.

By Mayank Gola, TrendTurtles  ·  10 min read  ·  Published: October 2026

In 2010, 494 farmers in Kallakurichi district, Tamil Nadu, pledged about 1,800 sovereigns of gold jewellery with cooperative societies, the local bodies that lend to farmers. A sovereign is 8 grams of gold, and pledging means handing it over as security for a loan. Many pledged it for their children's weddings, or to cover losses from crop damage. When they went to take it back, they were told it had been stolen, a farmers' union leader said. In January 2026, hundreds of farmers protested in Chennai, saying gold and money were missing from at least 75 cooperative societies, The News Minute reported. These are the farmers' allegations; no court has ruled on them yet.

Some frauds are huge. Aarudhra Gold Trading, a company in Tamil Nadu, promised people monthly returns of 10% to 30%. More than one lakh people put their money in. Police records show that ₹1,404 crore of it was never paid back. In all, 1,04,433 people have asked for about ₹2,438 crore back, the Free Press Journal reported in November 2025. The Economic Offences Wing of Tamil Nadu Police, which handles big money frauds, has filed a chargesheet, a formal list of charges, against 40 people and firms.

These are not accidents. In every case below, someone planned the trick in advance.

Gold Fraud: The Six Ways Buyers Are Cheated

The cases we found fall into six ways:

  1. Fake hallmarks. Low-gold jewellery carries a stamp that says 18 or 22 carat.
  2. Fake trading apps. A website or app shows your money growing, then asks for "tax" before you can take it out.
  3. Vanishing gold schemes. A company promises high returns or cheap gold, collects money, and stops paying.
  4. Pledged gold taken. Gold you pledged for a loan is swapped, re-pledged or not returned.
  5. Gold left with the jeweller. You leave gold for remaking or "hallmarking later", and the jeweller keeps it.
  6. Making charge and wastage billed higher than promised. The making charge is the cost of making the piece, and wastage is the gold the shop says is lost. You are told one rate and billed another.

All six work because you trust someone without checking.

One Real Case for Each Way

1. Fake hallmarks: a chain stamped 18 carat had about 10% gold (Kanpur, 2026)

In October 2026, a woman came to a jewellery shop in Kanpur to sell a gold chain and two rings. The chain carried an 18-carat hallmark, which means 75% gold. A test found only about 10% gold. The shop's manager called the police, and police later seized a hallmarking machine from a shop in the city, Amar Ujala reported.

2. Fake trading apps: a trader lost ₹12 crore (Jaipur, 2025)

In 2025, a gems trader in Jaipur got a WhatsApp link from someone calling herself Kritika Thakur. The link opened a website that claimed high returns on gold trading. He put in ₹5 lakh, then about ₹6 crore in instalments. When he tried to take money out, he was told 30% would be cut as "income tax". Over several payments, he lost ₹12 crore in all, ETV Bharat reported.

In simple words: the screen shows your money growing, and the "tax" to withdraw it is the last trick.

3. Vanishing schemes: "Where is the money?" (Heera Gold, 2024)

Heera Gold Exim collected money from the public with a promise to pay a 36% dividend, a share of profit, on the money invested. When it stopped paying, investors in several states complained of cheating. In October 2024, the Supreme Court cancelled the bail of its managing director. Bail is the court's permission to stay out of jail during a case. The court acted because she failed to raise ₹580 crore to pay investors' claims. During the hearing, a judge asked her lawyer: "Where is the money?"

There is also a simple test for a jeweller's own savings scheme. A legal explainer on LiveLaw says a jeweller's scheme that goes beyond 365 days could be treated as an unregulated deposit, which the law bans. In simple words: a jeweller's scheme should end within a year.

4. Pledged gold taken or swapped: 176 loan accounts at one bank (Andhra Pradesh, 2026, alleged)

In 2026, police arrested the gold appraiser at a Bank of Baroda branch in Eluru district, Andhra Pradesh. An appraiser is the person who checks and values the gold you pledge. He is accused of taking pledged gold from 176 gold loan accounts, worth about ₹10 crore. A check of the vault found it. Police have recovered ₹5.81 crore so far. In Kakinada, police arrested a former appraiser of the same bank in October 2026. He is accused of replacing customers' pledged gold with artificial ornaments, about 1,057 grams worth ₹1.45 crore.

It also happens at cooperative societies. In Thoothukudi, Tamil Nadu, police arrested four more people in October 2026 in an alleged ₹29 crore fraud at a cooperative society between 2019 and 2021. Police found that members' gold was taken from the society and pledged again at other banks, in the names of more than 10 people, including a car driver and domestic workers.

5. Gold left with the jeweller: "we will hallmark it later" (Gwalior, 2026, alleged)

Mansingh Gurjar's son was getting married in February. He had 640 grams of gold jewellery made by a jeweller in Gwalior. In the wedding rush, the jeweller gave it without a hallmark and said he would hallmark it later. After the wedding, Gurjar took the jewellery back to him. The jeweller said the server was down, kept the jewellery and gave him a receipt. Two days later, the shop was shut and the jeweller was gone, NPG News reported in June 2026.

Once he went to the police, dozens of others came forward who had been cheated the same way. The jeweller and his brother are accused of taking more than 3.5 kg of gold, worth about ₹4 crore. Police arrested them on the Meerut highway.

A receipt cannot stop a jeweller who runs away, but it is your proof. In Kangra, Himachal Pradesh, a jeweller denied receiving 32 grams of gold given for remaking in 2023. The customer had a receipt, and a consumer court ordered the jeweller to return the gold and pay ₹35,000.

6. Making charge and wastage: promised 3–5%, billed 23.5% (Bangalore, 2018)

A government teacher in Bangalore was promised 3–5% wastage in a jeweller's gold scheme. Her bill showed 23.5%. She paid ₹78,300 for a necklace that should have cost ₹71,000, and a consumer court ordered the jeweller to pay her back, Indian Jeweller reported.

What It Costs Families

It takes the gold meant for a wedding. Many of the Tamil Nadu farmers pledged their gold for their children's weddings. Mansingh Gurjar's jewellery was for his son's wedding. The farmers' union says many farmers have died by suicide over this.

It takes years of savings. Over one lakh people have claimed money from Aarudhra. In October 2024, Heera Gold's investors were still waiting for ₹580 crore.

You find out late. The farmers learnt of their loss when they went to take their gold back. In 2024, LocalCircles, a citizen survey platform, wrote that many buyers learn their gold is less pure than they paid for when they try to remake or sell it.

What To Do Before You Pay, Pledge or Invest

There are seven steps, one for each trick and one for when it goes wrong.

  1. Check the hallmark code before you pay. BIS, the Bureau of Indian Standards, is the government body that runs hallmarking. Type the six-character hallmark code, called the HUID, into its free BIS Care app. If you doubt the gold later, a BIS-recognised centre tests it for ₹200.
  2. Never pay to take your money out. If an app or website asks for "tax" or a fee before you can withdraw, stop. It is the sign of a fake platform.
  3. Join only a scheme that ends within a year. Get a receipt for every payment. A promise of returns like Heera Gold's 36% is a warning, not an offer.
  4. When you pledge gold with a bank or a finance company, know your rights. The RBI is the body that runs banks. Under the RBI's 2025 rules for gold loans, the lender must return your gold within seven working days after you repay. If the delay is its fault, it must pay you ₹5,000 for each day late. If your gold is lost or found less pure, the lender must compensate you. Check your gold when it is returned.
  5. Do not take unhallmarked pieces on a promise to hallmark them later. If you leave gold with a jeweller, get a receipt that shows the weight and what is to be made.
  6. Get every charge in writing before you pay. Ask for the making charge and wastage on the bill, each on its own line.
  7. If it goes wrong, complain at once. Call the National Consumer Helpline on 1915. For a fraud, go to the police. For a jeweller or a lender, you can also go to a consumer court.

Who needs this most:

  • A family buying for a wedding. Steps 1, 5 and 6. Mansingh Gurjar's loss began in a wedding rush.
  • Someone saving in a scheme or an app. Steps 2 and 3. Both tricks start with a promise of high returns.
  • A farmer or family pledging gold for a loan. Step 4. The seven-day rule covers banks and finance companies.
  • Anyone who has already lost gold. Step 7. The Kangra and Bangalore buyers won in a consumer court.

Key Takeaways

  • Over 1 lakh people lost ₹1,404 crore invested in one gold company, Tamil Nadu Police records show. In all, they have asked for about ₹2,438 crore back.
  • Most gold frauds are planned before you pay, in six ways: fake hallmarks, fake trading apps, vanishing schemes, pledged gold taken or swapped, gold left with the jeweller, and wastage billed higher than promised.
  • Each trick works because you trust someone without checking, and each has a warning sign.
  • Never pay a "tax" to withdraw your money, and never join a scheme longer than a year.
  • A bank or finance company must return pledged gold within seven working days, or pay ₹5,000 a day if the delay is its fault, under RBI rules.
  • Keep every receipt and bill. A receipt won the Kangra case.
