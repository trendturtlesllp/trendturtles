By Mayank Gola, TrendTurtles  ·  9 min read  ·  Published: October 2026  ·  Updated: 20 October 2026

This article is part of our series on how gold buyers are cheated. The main article shows all six ways. In this one, someone puts a false purity mark on gold on purpose: a seller, a hallmarking centre, or the person testing your gold.

In October 2026, police in Kanpur stopped a woman who was allegedly trying to sell a gold chain. The chain carried an 18-carat hallmark. An 18-carat piece should be 75% gold. A test found only about 10% gold in it. Citing police sources, The420 reports that 25 to 30 machines in the city may be used to print fake hallmarks.

Four people were held for questioning. By late that night, police said no one had been arrested yet, Amar Ujala reported.

A hallmark is meant to be the proof you can trust. When the mark itself is fake, you pay for gold you never got.

What a Real Gold Hallmark Looks Like

The hallmark comes from the Bureau of Indian Standards (BIS), the government body that sets quality standards. Since July 2021, BIS says a hallmark has three marks:

  • The BIS logo.
  • The purity. Karat measures how pure gold is, and 24 karat is pure gold. 22K916 means 22 karat, which is 91.6% gold. 18K750 means 18 karat, which is 75% gold.
  • A six-character code of letters and numbers, called the HUID. Every piece has its own.

If one of the three is missing, it is not a complete hallmark. The marks are small, so ask the jeweller to show you all three under a magnifying glass.

Some gold does not need a hallmark at all. Hallmarking is compulsory in 380 districts as of March 2026, not in every district. Even there, BIS lists cases where no hallmark is needed. Three of them matter to an ordinary buyer:

  • A piece that weighs less than two grams, such as a small nose pin.
  • Kundan, Polki and Jadau jewellery.
  • A jeweller whose sales are up to ₹40 lakh a year.

So a piece without a hallmark can be legal. But you cannot check its purity yourself. Where you have a choice, pick the piece that has one.

How a Fake Hallmark Reaches Your Gold

The cases below show four ways it happens:

  • A seller stamps the mark on low-gold jewellery. The stamp says 18 or 22 carat, and the metal is far less.
  • A hallmarking centre prints fake marks. The place meant to test the gold makes the false stamp itself.
  • The person running the test changes the result. This happens when you sell or exchange old gold, and the shop's machine decides the price.
  • A fake certificate comes with the piece. The seller shows you a printed paper instead of a BIS hallmark.

What Buyers Went Through

1. A buyer in Kashmir complained, and the shopkeeper was arrested (2025)

In January 2025, a buyer went to police in Srinagar. He said the gold ornaments he bought from a shop in Anantnag district were fake. Police arrested the shopkeeper. Police said he admitted to "embossing fake hallmarks on gold-like ornaments". In simple words: he stamped fake hallmarks on jewellery that only looked like gold. A laboratory test confirmed the ornaments were fake.

2. Fake BIS marks found at a hallmarking centre (2026)

In August 2026, BIS raided a hallmarking centre in Kadapa, Andhra Pradesh. It seized a laser machine that was allegedly used to print fake BIS hallmarks. It also seized a pen drive with fake BIS marks. The 72.7 grams of jewellery it found had false marks and no HUID.

For a first offence, the BIS law allows up to one year in jail, a fine of at least ₹1 lakh, or both. The fine can go up to five times the value of the goods.

3. A 22-carat bracelet tested as 20 carat, until the machine was changed (2026, alleged)

In August 2026, a customer went to a large jewellery showroom in Bilaspur. He wanted to exchange a 22-carat bracelet with a HUID. A jeweller from the shop that sold the bracelet went with him. According to the jeweller's complaint, the showroom's machine showed 20 carat two or three times. Then the jeweller said who he was. After that, another employee allegedly changed a setting on the machine. The next test showed 916, which is 22 carat.

Police filed a case (an FIR) against the manager and staff, The Hitavada reported. No court has found anyone guilty. In simple words: when a shop buys your old gold, its machine decides the price. A lower reading means less money for you.

4. ₹6 crore paid for jewellery worth ₹300, with a fake certificate (2022, alleged)

In 2022, an American tourist paid about ₹6 crore for jewellery in Jaipur. In 2024, experts at an exhibition in her country found it was fake, worth about ₹300. The sellers had allegedly given her a fake certificate saying the jewellery was genuine. Gold that should have been 14 carat was 2 carat, Business Today reported. Police arrested a man for allegedly issuing fake hallmark certificates.

5. "22-carat" bangles were 15.5 carat, and a court fined the jeweller (bought 2000, order 2018)

In 2000, a man in Chandigarh bought bangles and a chain sold as 22 carat. A lab in Delhi later found the bangles were 15.5 carat and the chain 19.5 carat. Under a thin layer of gold, the bangles had silver, copper and zinc.

In 2018, a consumer court fined the jeweller ₹2.15 lakh. It also ordered ₹5 lakh as punishment, to be paid into a fund for consumers' legal aid. The jeweller had to replace the jewellery or refund its current price, The Tribune reported.

How Often This Happens

We could not find a national count. Two official figures, from two regions, show how big it can be:

  • Tamil Nadu: BIS checked 8,580 jewellery shops between January 2023 and April 2025. It seized about 21.21 kg of jewellery, worth ₹19.27 crore, that had fake hallmarks or no proper hallmark. The figures came out through a Right to Information request, The Federal reported. That law lets any citizen ask a government office for facts.
  • Kashmir: in September 2025, the head of BIS in Jammu and Kashmir spoke about fake hallmarks in the wedding season. He said: "I can confirm that around 10 per cent of jewellers are involved". In simple words: about one jeweller in ten there was selling gold with fake hallmarks.

An 18-carat piece should be 75% gold. The Kanpur chain had about 10%.

What a Fake Hallmark Costs You

You pay for gold that is not there. With the Kanpur chain, a buyer would pay for 75% gold and get about 10%. Most of the money would buy nothing.

You find out late. In May 2024, LocalCircles, a citizen survey platform, wrote that buyers complain of "being charged for 22 carat gold jewellery whereas in reality it was just 18 carat gold". Many found out only when they tried to remake or sell it. In Chandigarh, the court order came 18 years after the bangles were bought.

A fake mark is easy to miss. In April 2025, LocalCircles asked people who had bought gold jewellery in the past year how long the code on it was. It got 29,853 answers. Only 18% said six characters, which is correct. 27% named a wrong length. 28% said there was no code, and 27% could not say. Only the platform's registered members answered, so it is not a count of all buyers. Still, a buyer who does not know the code cannot tell a real mark from a fake one.

You can lose again when you sell. If the shop's test reads your gold lower than it is, as alleged in Bilaspur, you get less money for real gold.

What To Do Before and After You Pay

There are six steps. You can do the first two in the shop, before you pay.

  1. Look for the three marks. Ask for a magnifying glass. You need the BIS logo, the purity (such as 22K916) and the six-character code.
  2. Check the code in BIS Care. It is a free app from BIS. Choose "Verify HUID" and type the code. BIS says this checks that the hallmark is real. If the app cannot find the code, do not buy.
  3. Ask for a full bill. It should show the purity, the weight, the code, the making charge and GST (the tax), each on its own line. Keep it, so you have a record of what you were sold.
  4. Do not accept a private certificate in place of a hallmark. A paper from the shop is not the government's mark.
  5. When you sell or exchange, watch the test. Ask for the purity and weight in writing. If the reading looks low, say you will get it tested at a BIS-recognised centre first.
  6. If you have doubts after you buy, get it tested for ₹200. BIS says anyone can have hallmarked jewellery tested at a BIS-recognised hallmarking centre for ₹200. Is the gold less pure than its mark? Then BIS rules say you are owed two times the value of the missing gold. You also get the testing charges back.

Who needs this most:

  • A family buying for a wedding. It is the biggest gold bill most families pay. Do steps 1 to 3 on every piece, not just the big ones.
  • Someone exchanging old gold. Step 5 matters most for you, because the shop's test sets the money you get.
  • A first-time or gift buyer. Steps 1 and 2 tell you the piece is what the tag says.
  • Anyone with hallmarked gold bought years ago. Step 6 works on old hallmarked pieces too.

Key Takeaways

  • In Kanpur in 2026, a chain with an 18-carat hallmark had about 10% gold.
  • Fake marks come from sellers and from hallmarking centres. In Bilaspur, showroom staff are accused of changing a test result at exchange.
  • A real hallmark has three marks: the BIS logo, the purity, and a six-character code.
  • The free BIS Care app checks that code before you pay.
  • A BIS-recognised centre tests hallmarked jewellery for ₹200. If it is less pure than its mark, you are owed two times the value of the missing gold.
  • Keep the bill. It is your record of what you were sold, even years later.
