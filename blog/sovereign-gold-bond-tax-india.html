Sovereign Gold Bonds: "Secondary-Market Buyers Now Face 12.5% LTCG at Maturity"

If you bought your gold bond on the stock exchange, you now pay 12.5% tax on your gain when it matures. This started on 1 April 2026.

Value Research, 5 June 2026. Source: Value Research, an Indian firm that rates mutual funds and stocks.

By Mayank Gola, TrendTurtles  ·  9 min read  ·  Published: October 2026

A Sovereign Gold Bond is a bond from the government. Its value goes up and down with the price of gold. It pays 2.5% interest a year, and it ends after eight years. That end is called maturity. On that day, you are paid in cash.

Your gain is what you get at the end, minus what you paid. For years, people liked one thing about these bonds most. The gain at maturity was free of tax.

There are two ways to own a gold bond:

  • At issue. You bought it when the government first sold it, through your bank, a post office or a broker.
  • From the stock exchange. You bought it later from another person, through an app. The NSE and BSE are India's two stock exchanges. This is the "secondary market".

Until March 2026, most people believed both were tax-free at maturity. From 1 April 2026, only the first one is. "LTCG" means long-term capital gains tax. It is the tax on your gain, when you have held the bond for more than a year.

Many people still think every gold bond is tax-free at the end. It is not anymore. This is part of our series on gold rules that are unclear or changed midway. See also the rule on gold at home and digital gold, which no regulator watches.

Sovereign Gold Bond Tax: What Changed on 1 April 2026

The Budget is the government's yearly money plan. The 2026 Budget changed one line in the tax law. Moneylife explains it. Now the tax-free maturity needs two things at once:

  1. You bought the bond when it was first issued.
  2. You kept it until maturity, without a break.

Miss either one, and you pay tax on your gain.

After five years, you can hand the bond back to the RBI on set dates. The RBI is the Reserve Bank of India, which runs these bonds. This is called the early exit. Moneylife says one more thing. Some people bought at issue but did not keep the bond to the end. They sold it early, or used the early exit. They pay tax too.

This applies to bonds people already own. It is not only for new ones. You cannot change how you got your bond. Value Research says so.

The tax department sees it another way. Moneylife reports that it calls this a clarification. It says the tax-free rule was never meant for bonds bought from another person.

One thing did not change. The 2.5% interest was always taxed, for everyone.

How you got the bondTax on your gain at maturity, from 1 April 2026
At issue, and kept until maturityNo tax
At issue, but sold early or used the early exitTax on your gain (Moneylife)
From the stock exchange, kept for more than a year12.5% of your gain

What Gold Bond Holders Went Through

Here are five real cases and warnings. Each has a link, so you can read it yourself.

1. They bought on the exchange to get tax-free gold. That ended.

Older gold bonds often sold on the exchange for less than their gold was worth. Value Research says a buyer could get them cheap. They kept them to the end. They paid no tax on the gain. The new rule closed that door.

Not everyone thinks this was fair. Harsh Roongta runs an investment advice firm. It is registered with SEBI, the market watchdog. He wrote in Business Standard in February 2026: "In cricket, this would be called unfair play. In taxation, it is called a retrospective change." In simple words: the rule changed for bonds people had already bought. That is his view.

2. No new bonds. The exchange is now the only way in.

In February 2025, the Finance Minister was asked if the scheme was ending. She said, "Yes, in a way." The last new issue was in February 2023.

Put that next to the new tax rule. If you buy a gold bond today, you can only buy it on the exchange. So you will pay tax on your gain at maturity.

3. Some holders' interest was not being paid.

In October 2024, Punjab National Bank put out a public request. It asked its own gold bond customers to visit their branch within five days. It meant most of all those whose interest was not being paid. It also said: "In case of a wrong account number or closed account number, bank will not be liable for any delay." In simple words: if the account is wrong or closed, the bank will not take the blame for late money.

The bank asked holders not to close that bank account until the bond ends. If they must close it, they should give a new account number first. If they do not, the interest and maturity money "will not be credited".

In simple words: your bond pays into one bank account. Close it without telling anyone, and the money has nowhere to go.

And it does not wait for you forever. The same Business Standard report gives the RBI's rule: "Any amount that remains unclaimed for over seven years should be transferred to the IPEF". In simple words: money nobody claims for seven years goes into a government fund. You can still get it back, but only by making a valid claim.

4. An investor thought he had lost money. He had not.

In 2022, a gold bond investor wrote to Outlook Money. His account showed his bond worth less than he paid, while gold had gone up. He wrote: "I may have been better off buying gold from my local store, as I know the owner."

Outlook Money explained why. The price on the exchange is not the price at maturity. Very few gold bonds change hands each day. So their price on the exchange often stays below gold. At maturity, the government pays the real price of gold.

So he likely had nothing to fear if he held on. But it shows one more thing people do not know. If you need to sell early on the exchange, you may get less than the gold is worth.

5. Miss the early-exit date, and you wait.

After five years, you can leave early, but only on dates the RBI sets for each bond. Business Today says each bond has a set number of days to send the request. Missing them "means waiting for the next eligible redemption cycle". In simple words: you wait for the next date.

The Impact: Same Bond, Same Gold, Different Tax

Here is an example with round numbers. It shows how the rule works. It is not anyone's real case.

Two people hold the same gold bond. Each makes a gain of ₹1 lakh at maturity.

  • Person A bought it at issue and kept it. Tax on the gain: ₹0.
  • Person B bought it on the exchange. Tax on the gain at 12.5%: about ₹12,500.

Same bond. Same gold. The only difference is where they bought it. Moneylife says the change hits people who bought on the exchange. Many of them used apps like Zerodha or Groww.

The tax is only part of it. A closed bank account can hold up your money. A missed date means you wait for the next one. None of these problems came from the price of gold. They came from a rule the holder did not know.

What To Do If You Hold a Gold Bond

Six steps. Most take a few minutes.

  1. Find out how you got each bond. Open your bond statement from your bank or your broker's app. See if each bond was bought at issue or on the exchange. Value Research says this is the first thing to check.
  2. Bought at issue? Think before you exit early. Held to the end, your gain is free of tax. Sell early or use the early exit, and you may pay tax.
  3. Bought on the exchange? Plan for the tax. Keep 12.5% of your expected gain aside for the day the bond ends. Value Research suggests you also check if holding to the end still makes sense for you.
  4. Keep open the bank account your bond pays into. Changed your bank? Tell your bank, post office or broker the new account number now.
  5. Write down your early-exit dates. If you may need the money, ask your bank or broker for your bond's exit dates. The RBI publishes them. Send your request within the set days.
  6. Name a nominee. A nominee is the person who gets the bond if you die. Under RBI's rules, a nominee can claim it from the office where you bought it. Without one, your family first needs court papers that prove who gets the bond.

Who needs this most:

  • You bought gold bonds through an app like Zerodha or Groww. Do steps 1 and 3. Your bond is no longer tax-free at the end.
  • You bought at your bank years ago and are now retired. Do steps 2, 4 and 6. Your tax-free gain is safe if you hold on.
  • You changed your bank or closed an old account. Do step 4 today.

Key Takeaways

  • From 1 April 2026, people who bought gold bonds on the stock exchange pay 12.5% tax on their gain at maturity.
  • People who bought at issue and keep the bond to the end still pay no tax on the gain.
  • The change applies to bonds people already own.
  • No new gold bonds are being sold, so every new buyer now buys on the exchange.
  • Keep open the bank account your bond pays into. If you close it without giving a new one, your money will not be credited.
